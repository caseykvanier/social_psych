# Chapter 1: Introducing Social Psychology

## What this chapter is about

Social psychology studies how people think, feel, and behave in relation to other people. The chapter connects three levels of explanation: characteristics of the person, features of the social situation, and the ongoing influence between them. It then introduces the scientific methods used to test social-psychological explanations. The central lesson is that plausible stories about behaviour are not enough; claims must be tied to observable evidence and to methods capable of supporting the conclusion being drawn.

## Slide 3 - Two questions about clothing

### What the slide shows

A person in a bikini walks along a dock beside two questions: whether it is acceptable to go outside in a bikini and whether it is acceptable to go outside in underwear.

### Detailed notes

The bikini and underwear may cover similar portions of the body, yet they carry different social meanings. A swimsuit fits the shared expectations of a beach or pool; underwear usually violates them. The physical behaviour therefore cannot be interpreted apart from its context. The instructor’s pool example shows that even functionally similar garments may receive different reactions because a community has categorized one as appropriate swimwear and the other as inappropriate.

### Examples and applications

This comparison introduces a **social norm**: an understood rule about appropriate behaviour in a situation. Norms need not be formally written or logically necessary to shape reactions.

### Key takeaway

The meaning of a behaviour depends partly on the social situation and the norms attached to it.

## Slide 4 - Definition of social psychology

### What the slide shows

Large text defines the field as the scientific study of how people feel about, think about, and behave toward the people around them. The words “feel,” “think,” “behave,” and “people” are visually emphasized.

### Detailed notes

Each part of the definition matters. The subject is social because it concerns people in relation to others. It includes feelings, thoughts, and actions rather than reducing social life to behaviour alone. It is scientific because explanations are evaluated through a falsifiable process: develop a hypothesis, choose a method, gather evidence, and ask whether the result supports or challenges the hypothesis. A failed prediction can require a better method, a revised hypothesis, or a revised theory.

Everyday life also produces explanations for why people act as they do. What distinguishes social psychology is not merely having a theory, but subjecting it to systematic evidence and remaining open to being wrong.

### Key takeaway

Social psychology applies scientific methods to affect, cognition, and behaviour in social contexts.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 00:09-00:14._

## Slide 5 - Social influence

### What the slide shows

Text defines social influence as the process through which people change one another’s thoughts, feelings, and behaviours. A photograph shows a child mirroring an adult’s crossed-arm posture.

### Detailed notes

Influence is reciprocal. Parents deliberately shape children, children imitate adults, friends shape one another’s opinions and conduct, and each person also changes the groups they join. Moving between groups may emphasize different aspects of the same person. Someone may therefore prefer a group not only because of its members, but because they like the version of themselves that emerges in that setting.

### Key takeaway

People continuously influence others while also being changed by them.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 00:13-00:17._

## Slide 6 - Behaviour as a function of person and situation

### What the slide shows

The expression `behaviour = f(person, social situation)` appears above icons representing an individual and settings such as school, family, and religion.

### Detailed notes

To predict behaviour, social psychologists consider both the individual and the situation. People differ in personality, history, attitudes, expectations, and skills. Situations also differ in their roles and rules. The same student may be quiet, attentive, and raise a hand in class but behave very differently with friends; the classroom activates “student” thoughts, feelings, and conduct. Conversely, different students placed in the same classroom may adopt different roles, such as a highly reserved participant or a class clown.

The relationship is an interaction, not a competition between two explanations. A setting brings out tendencies in a person, and a person’s behaviour alters the setting and the reactions received from others.

### Key takeaway

Behaviour reflects an interaction between who the person is and the situation they enter.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 00:15-00:23._

## Slide 7 - Evolutionary adaptation

### What the slide shows

A sequence presents three conditions for evolution: social behaviours are heritable, social behaviours vary, and some variants provide better fitness.

### Detailed notes

The lecture introduces evolution as one possible source of social tendencies. Genes contribute not only to bodies but also to brains and behavioural predispositions. Traits such as aggression can vary partly for heritable reasons, while experience and context also shape their expression. Extremely high aggression may provoke dangerous conflict, whereas an absolute lack of assertiveness may make it difficult to protect resources. Selection can therefore favour tendencies that work reasonably well in a recurring environment rather than an extreme at either end.

In this context, **fitness** means reproductive success across generations, not moral worth or physical fitness. Evolutionary explanation does not mean that a behaviour is inevitable, optimal today, or justified.

### Key takeaway

Heritable variation in social tendencies can be shaped by reproductive consequences, but behaviour still depends on experience and context.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 00:23-00:31._

## Slide 8 - Self-concern, kin selection, and ingroups

### What the slide shows

A wedding image is overlaid with the terms “self-concern,” “kin selection,” and “ingroups,” moving from the individual toward family and broader groups.

### Detailed notes

**Self-concern** describes attention to one’s own welfare. **Kin selection** extends evolutionary reasoning to relatives who share some genetic material: helping relatives can indirectly help shared genes persist. **Ingroups** are groups to which a person feels they belong, including families, friends, teams, schools, or communities.

The instructor uses an arbitrary “team red” and “team blue” division to show how little may be required to create ingroup favouritism and negative assumptions about an outgroup. The categories need not reflect meaningful differences to shape judgment. People often become more willing to help as another person is categorized closer to the self, family, or valued group.

### Key takeaway

People organize social concern around the self, relatives, and groups, and even arbitrary group boundaries can affect evaluation.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 00:29-00:35._

## Slide 9 - Affiliation as a motive

### What the slide shows

A collage of cooperative and social activities surrounds the statement that affiliation with others is a fundamental motivator serving several purposes.

### Detailed notes

**Affiliation** means connection or belonging. Loneliness is presented not simply as being physically alone, but as an unpleasant signal that desired connection is missing. Because affiliation is motivational, it can produce action: people seek new relationships, repair threatened ones, or work to prove that they belong.

The lecture uses fraternity and sorority recruitment as an example. Applicants may network, undergo rituals, and follow group norms because membership is valuable. These efforts also create an ingroup boundary: successful candidates can display membership, while other groups become comparison outgroups.

### Key takeaway

The need to belong motivates people to seek, maintain, and sometimes work hard for group membership.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 00:33-00:39._

## Slide 10 - Social norms in everyday life

### What the slide shows

A black-and-white street scene shows people lined up for public transit. A caption identifies queuing as an example of a social norm.

### Detailed notes

Social norms are shared expectations about appropriate conduct. A person who cuts a transit line may violate no criminal law but still receive disapproval. Returning a shopping cart and taking turns in conversation work similarly: compliance is encouraged through subtle approval, while violations may provoke dirty looks, criticism, exclusion, or loss of future interaction.

Norms vary between cultures and settings. Queuing may be expected in one city while crowding toward a bus is routine in another. A newcomer can therefore appear rude without intending harm simply because the local rule was unknown. The discomfort of entering an unfamiliar setting often reflects an effort to discover and follow its norms.

### Key takeaway

Norms coordinate social life through informal expectations and consequences that differ across settings and cultures.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 00:38-00:48._

## Slide 11 - Individualism, collectivism, and cultural norms

### What the slide shows

A colour-coded map of Europe is labelled as a map of individualism, with a scale ranging from more collectivist to more individualist cultures. The slide states that different cultures have different norms.

### Detailed notes

**Individualism** emphasizes personal goals, autonomy, and becoming one’s own distinctive self. **Collectivism** emphasizes the welfare and expectations of valued groups such as family or community. The lecture contrasts choosing a career mainly to maximize personal opportunity with choosing one because a community needs that role.

These are tendencies, not two types of people or countries. Every person pursues some individual aims and participates in collective obligations. Cultures and groups differ in which orientation they emphasize, and people may experience tension when family expectations reflect a more collectivist orientation than the surrounding society.

The map is an illustrative cultural comparison, not evidence that every individual in a coloured region shares one outlook. Country-level averages must not be treated as complete descriptions of individuals.

### Key takeaway

Cultures differ in the relative emphasis placed on individual goals and group welfare, but both orientations exist within every society.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 00:45-00:52._

## Slide 12 - Affect, behaviour, and cognition

### What the slide shows

A three-part circle divides interaction with others into affect, behaviour, and cognition.

### Detailed notes

The ABC components mutually influence one another. Feelings change what people notice and how they act; thoughts shape emotional interpretations and planned actions; behaviour can create new feelings and thoughts. For example, beginning a difficult conversation may change an initially anxious interpretation once the interaction goes well.

The lecture connects this reciprocal structure to cognitive-behavioural therapy: changing patterns of thought or practising new behaviours can alter emotional experience. The point is not that one component always comes first, but that intervention at one point can influence the others.

### Key takeaway

Affect, behaviour, and cognition operate as an interacting system rather than three independent causes.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 00:51-00:57._

## Slide 13 - Social cognition

### What the slide shows

Two figures with thought bubbles illustrate the definition: social cognition is cognition related to social activities.

### Detailed notes

Social cognition includes thinking about relationships, roles, impressions, expectations, and appropriate conduct. It often operates without deliberate awareness, yet it can require substantial mental effort. A social evening may be tiring because a person is tracking who likes whom, choosing what to say, following conversational norms, maintaining relationships, and presenting an intended image of the self.

### Key takeaway

Social cognition is the mental work involved in understanding and navigating social life.

## Slide 14 - What we know and feel about doctors

### What the slide shows

A photograph of a medical coat and stethoscope appears behind two questions: “What do you know about doctors?” and “How do you feel about doctors?”

### Detailed notes

The first question elicits factual or categorical knowledge: training, work settings, income, and professional duties. The second elicits affect, which can vary with a person’s history. A medical professional may evoke admiration, fear, embarrassment, or concern about being judged. Replacing “doctor” with “dentist” or “psychiatrist” may activate a different emotional response even though all are health professions.

This distinction shows that knowledge and feeling are related but not identical. A social role carries both a cognitive representation and an attitude.

### Key takeaway

People can hold factual knowledge and a separate emotional attitude toward the same social category.

## Slide 15 - Schemas and attitudes

### What the slide shows

Text defines a schema as a knowledge representation about a person or group and an attitude as a representation centred mainly on liking or disliking a person, object, or group.

### Detailed notes

A **schema** is a framework for organizing information. A dog schema, for example, contains typical features such as fur, four legs, barking, and familiar behaviours. Some examples fit a schema more prototypically than others, yet category membership is not determined solely by resemblance to the prototype.

Schemas create expectations and make new information easier to interpret, but they also direct attention toward information that confirms what is already believed. **Attitudes** add evaluation: favourable or unfavourable feeling toward the represented object. Political-party examples illustrate how people may notice favourable evidence about their preferred group and negative evidence about an opposing group.

### Key takeaway

Schemas organize what people know; attitudes organize how positively or negatively they evaluate it.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 01:00-01:06._

## Slide 16 - Affect, mood, and emotion

### What the slide shows

Pairs of expressive faces accompany a distinction: mood is a background state, while emotion is brief but often intense.

### Detailed notes

In psychology, **affect** is a noun referring broadly to emotional state. A mood can persist in the background across a longer period, whereas an emotion is usually a more focused, short-lived reaction. Both can alter cognition and behaviour. This psychological use of “affect” differs from the everyday verb “to affect,” meaning to influence, and from the noun “effect,” meaning a result.

### Key takeaway

Affect includes longer-lasting moods and briefer, often more intense emotions.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 01:06-01:08._

## Slide 17 - Social exchange

### What the slide shows

Two people are linked by arrows carrying goods, services, emotions, and other social outcomes in both directions.

### Detailed notes

Many relationships unfold as repeated exchanges rather than isolated encounters. What happened in one interaction affects expectations in the next. Balanced exchanges tend to feel fair, while a relationship in which one person repeatedly takes without reciprocating may feel exploitative.

Trust is an expectation about future exchange. A friend may receive help now without immediate repayment because both people expect the relationship to continue. Repeated positive interactions build increasingly favourable attitudes and confidence that support will eventually be returned.

### Key takeaway

Ongoing relationships are shaped by repeated exchanges and expectations of future reciprocity.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 01:08-01:11._

## Slide 18 - Reciprocal altruism

### What the slide shows

A photograph of people sharing food is paired with the idea that benefits to non-relatives can evolve when benefits are reciprocated in the future.

### Detailed notes

**Altruism** involves providing a benefit at some cost to oneself without receiving an immediate equivalent return. In reciprocal altruism, assistance is supported by an expectation that members of an ongoing relationship or community will help one another across time. A farmer with excess food may share it now and rely on neighbours during a later shortage.

Reciprocity depends on trust and continuity. When people expect no future interaction, they may demand an immediate, balanced transaction. When they identify with a durable community, they can tolerate temporary imbalance because support is evaluated over a longer horizon.

### Key takeaway

Trust and repeated interaction can support costly helping among people who are not relatives.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-26, approximately 01:10-01:12._

## Slide 19 - Social psychology as empirical research

### What the slide shows

Large text states that social psychology is about empirical research.

### Detailed notes

**Empirical** means grounded in systematic observation or measurement. Social psychologists begin with questions and hypotheses, choose methods that can generate evidence, and compare the evidence with their predictions. The process is iterative: results lead to revised questions, improved methods, and more precise theories.

### Key takeaway

Social-psychological claims must be evaluated against systematically gathered evidence.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:00-00:04._

## Slide 20 - Rigorous scientific investigation

### What the slide shows

A researcher silhouette accompanies a statement that interest in social behaviour without rigorous scientific investigation is not social psychology.

### Detailed notes

Intuition can generate a hypothesis, but it cannot by itself establish that the hypothesis is correct. Scientific claims must be falsifiable: the method must allow evidence that could count against the prediction. Researchers also need to distinguish a poor theory from a poorly designed study. Unexpected results may expose either problem and motivate the next investigation.

### Key takeaway

Social psychology requires methods that can test—and potentially disconfirm—an explanation.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:02-00:05._

## Slide 21 - Hindsight bias

### What the slide shows

A photograph of Donald Trump’s 2017 inauguration appears above the phrase “I knew that was going to happen.”

### Detailed notes

**Hindsight bias** is the tendency to see an outcome as more predictable after learning that it occurred. The lecture uses the unexpected result of the 2016 United States presidential election: after the result, it became easy to identify a chain of events that made the outcome seem obvious, even though many people had been surprised beforehand.

The investment example makes the same distinction. Knowing now that an earlier investment rose greatly does not mean that choosing it was clearly correct with the information available at the time. Prospective predictions are stronger tests than explanations assembled after the outcome is known.

### Key takeaway

Knowing an outcome changes how predictable the past appears, so retrospective certainty should not be confused with prior evidence.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:04-00:09._

## Slide 22 - Conceptual variables

### What the slide shows

A network-like icon of connected people accompanies the phrase “conceptual variables.”

### Detailed notes

A **conceptual variable** is the abstract phenomenon a researcher wants to understand, such as mood, boredom, prejudice, or affiliation. It is not observed directly. Researchers must decide what observable evidence will stand for the concept, and different choices may capture different parts of it.

### Key takeaway

Research begins with an abstract concept but needs a defensible way to represent it in observable data.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:09-00:11._

## Slide 23 - Boredom as a conceptual variable

### What the slide shows

Boredom is conceptually defined as an affective state involving lack of stimulation, low physiological arousal, and unpleasant feelings.

### Detailed notes

The definition specifies the construct of interest without yet determining how it will be measured. Different dimensions of boredom may not always move together: someone’s self-reported unpleasantness, outward expression, and physiological arousal can supply different information. A clear conceptual definition helps researchers explain what their measures are intended to represent.

### Key takeaway

A conceptual definition states what a construct means before a particular measurement method is chosen.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:10-00:13._

## Slide 24 - Operational definitions of boredom

### What the slide shows

The conceptual definition remains at the top, while three possible operational measures appear below: self-report, physiology, and behaviour.

### Detailed notes

An **operational definition** is the concrete procedure used to measure a variable. Boredom might be assessed with a questionnaire, physiological activity such as electroencephalography, or behaviour such as yawning and attention drift. These measures need not produce identical results because they access different aspects of the concept and have different sources of error.

Self-report may reveal private experience but can be influenced by memory, interpretation, and willingness to answer honestly. Behavioural coding may be relatively naturalistic but can be ambiguous. Physiological measures can be precise while still requiring interpretation and may change the situation merely by attaching equipment. Confidence increases when different methods converge on the same conclusion.

### Key takeaway

Operationalization determines what researchers actually observe, and no single measure is automatically identical to the concept itself.

## Slide 25 - Three research designs

### What the slide shows

A table compares observational, correlational, and experimental research. Observation is descriptive; correlation assesses relationships; experiments manipulate variables to test cause and effect. The table also contrasts their advantages and limitations.

### Detailed notes

**Observational research** records naturally occurring behaviour without deliberately changing it. It is useful for description and developing questions, and it can closely resemble everyday life. It cannot by itself establish relationships between unmeasured variables or causal direction.

**Correlational research** measures at least two variables and asks whether knowing one provides information about the other. It supports prediction and can examine real-world patterns, but correlation alone cannot determine which variable causes the other or whether a third variable explains both.

**Experimental research** manipulates an independent variable and measures its effect on a dependent variable. When groups are comparable and the manipulation is the only systematic difference, an experiment can support causal inference. Experiments may be expensive, artificial, unethical, or impossible for some questions, so the other designs remain essential.

### Key takeaway

Different designs answer different questions; only a well-controlled experiment can directly test cause and effect.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:15-00:30._

## Slide 26 - Reading correlations

### What the slide shows

Three scatterplots show a strong positive correlation (`r = .9`), a negative correlation (`r = -.7`), and no linear correlation (`r = 0`) between measured variables X and Y.

### Detailed notes

In a positive correlation, higher values of one variable tend to accompany higher values of the other. In a negative correlation, higher values of one tend to accompany lower values of the other. When `r` is near zero, a straight line does not summarize the points usefully. The sign gives direction, while the magnitude gives the strength of the linear relationship.

In a simple correlation, swapping X and Y does not change the correlation and does not establish a causal direction. A zero linear correlation also does not rule out every possible nonlinear relationship; it says there is no straight-line pattern of the type shown.

### Key takeaway

Correlation describes the direction and strength of a relationship between measured variables, not why the relationship exists.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:30-00:35._

## Slide 27 - Front-row seating and grades

### What the slide shows

An empty lecture hall is captioned with the claim that people who sit at the front of a class do better in school.

### Detailed notes

The claim can be translated into measurable variables such as distance from the instructor and course grade. If shorter distance tends to accompany higher grades, the variables are correlated. That pattern alone would not show that changing seats changes achievement because students choose their seats for many reasons.

### Key takeaway

A testable claim must be translated into variables, but a measured association is not yet a causal explanation.

## Slide 28 - Ambiguous causal direction

### What the slide shows

“Sit at the front” and “do better” are connected by arrows in both directions.

### Detailed notes

A correlation permits multiple causal stories. Sitting near the front could improve attention and performance, but highly motivated students could also choose front seats. With only seating and grades measured, the data cannot determine which direction is correct. The two-headed diagram warns against inserting a preferred causal story into correlational evidence.

### Key takeaway

When two variables correlate, either causal direction may be possible unless the design supplies stronger evidence.

## Slide 29 - Third-variable explanations

### What the slide shows

Interest, intelligence, and motivation point toward both front-row seating and better performance.

### Detailed notes

A **third variable** may cause or influence both measured variables. Interest could lead students to sit closer and study more; motivation could lead to earlier arrival and stronger academic effort; prior achievement could affect both confidence and grades. Unless these possibilities are measured or controlled, the observed relationship cannot be assigned to seating alone.

### Key takeaway

A correlation between two variables may be explained by an unmeasured factor affecting both.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:38-00:42._

## Slide 30 - Experimental manipulation

### What the slide shows

Students are assigned to sit in the front or back half of a class. Seating is labelled the manipulated independent variable and course grade the measured dependent variable.

### Detailed notes

An experiment changes seating rather than allowing students to select it. The **independent variable** is the condition manipulated by the researcher: front versus back. The **dependent variable** is the measured outcome expected to depend on that manipulation: course grade. If comparable groups differ after the manipulation, the design supplies evidence that seating position contributed to the difference.

Some questions cannot be manipulated ethically or practically. Researchers cannot randomly assign people to harmful life conditions. Studies using naturally occurring groups can still be valuable, but their causal conclusions require greater caution.

### Key takeaway

Experiments test causation by manipulating an independent variable and measuring a dependent outcome.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:40-00:43._

## Slide 31 - Internal validity

### What the slide shows

Front and back seating conditions appear beside the statement that the experimental manipulation should be the only difference between conditions.

### Detailed notes

**Internal validity** concerns whether the design supports the claimed cause-and-effect interpretation. If students with stronger prior grades are disproportionately assigned to the front, later differences might reflect prior achievement rather than seating. Researchers must keep procedures equivalent and prevent pre-existing group differences from becoming alternative explanations.

### Key takeaway

An experiment is internally valid when the manipulation, rather than a confound, explains the outcome difference.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:42-00:44._

## Slide 32 - Random assignment

### What the slide shows

Motivated and unmotivated participant icons are mixed and randomly assigned across front and back seating conditions.

### Detailed notes

**Random assignment** gives each participant a chance of entering either condition. It does not guarantee perfectly identical groups, especially in a small sample, but it prevents the researcher from systematically placing particular kinds of people into one condition. With adequate sample size, known and unknown participant characteristics should be distributed more evenly.

Random assignment to conditions differs from random sampling from a population. The former primarily supports internal validity; the latter primarily supports generalization.

### Key takeaway

Random assignment reduces systematic pre-existing differences between experimental groups.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:43-00:45._

## Slide 33 - Deception and debriefing

### What the slide shows

A person hides crossed fingers behind their back beside text explaining that researchers using a cover story or experimental confederate must thoroughly debrief participants.

### Detailed notes

Some social-psychological studies cannot reveal their full purpose in advance because knowledge of the hypothesis would change behaviour. A **confederate** appears to be another participant but follows a role designed by the researchers. The Milgram obedience study is used to illustrate why deception can be methodologically useful and ethically dangerous: participants had to believe the apparent shocks were real, but that belief could create serious distress.

Deception therefore requires strong justification, ethical review, minimization of harm, and a careful **debriefing** afterward. Debriefing explains the true purpose, identifies the deception, and helps participants understand what occurred. Scientific value does not remove the obligation to protect participants.

### Key takeaway

Deception may sometimes protect a study’s validity, but it creates ethical duties to minimize harm and fully debrief participants.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:45-00:50._

## Slide 34 - External validity

### What the slide shows

Two photographs contrast a controlled study setting with people in everyday life. External validity is defined as the extent to which a relationship found in one study holds with different people, in another study, or in the real world.

### Detailed notes

**External validity** concerns generalization beyond the specific study. A well-controlled laboratory result may not apply to another population, context, or time. The lecture uses treatments that work in mice but not necessarily in humans as a clear example. Social behaviour in an artificial room, while wearing measurement equipment and knowing one is observed, may also differ from ordinary behaviour.

Observation is not automatically free of this problem: a family may change its dinner behaviour when a researcher sits nearby with a clipboard. The replication crisis further demonstrates that small differences in samples, procedures, settings, and analysis can matter. Internal validity asks whether the study supports its causal conclusion; external validity asks how far that conclusion travels.

### Key takeaway

A study can be internally strong yet limited in how well its result generalizes to other people and settings.

_Lecture source: 2026-08-31, approximately 00:49-00:55._

## Lecture-only material - Designing studies of social-media likes

The August 31 class ends with a research-design activity not represented in the 34-slide PDF. Students are asked whether a post displaying 12,000 likes would be judged more positively than the identical post displaying 12 likes. They then design observational, correlational, and experimental ways to investigate the question.

The exercise brings the chapter together. An observational study could describe naturally occurring reactions but would not control the like count. A correlational study could measure displayed likes and judgments across posts but could not separate popularity from post quality or other third variables. An experiment could hold the post constant, manipulate only the displayed number of likes, randomly assign viewers, and measure their evaluation. That design would support the strongest causal claim that the displayed like count changed judgment.
