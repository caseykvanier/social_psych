# Chapter 7: Liking and Loving

*Draft lecture notes. Slide numbers follow the PDF. Recording times are approximate.*

## What this chapter is about

Attraction is not exclusively romantic. The chapter starts with how people become interested in one another, then asks how familiarity, shared values, support, and commitment turn some connections into close relationships. Explanations at different levels can coexist: a particular person may be easy to meet, familiar, and similar to us at the same time.

## Slide 1 - Close relationships

**What the slide shows:** A ring of possible benefits surrounds silhouettes of people together.

**Detailed notes:** A person can know many people while relying on a smaller circle of close friends, family, or partners. A close tie can provide emotional support (someone to listen when things are hard) and practical support (someone to accompany you to an appointment). Trust also creates vulnerability: what another person does can matter much more when you depend on them.

**Key takeaway:** Closeness can provide support and make us more vulnerable; it is not just frequent contact. *(October 28, first 15 minutes.)*

## Slide 2 - Interpersonal attraction

**What the slide shows:** A caregiver holding an infant illustrates a nonromantic connection.

**Detailed notes:** Interpersonal attraction concerns being drawn toward other people. The relationship may be familial, friendly, or romantic; evidence about partner preferences does not automatically generalize to all forms of liking.

**Key takeaway:** Distinguish general interpersonal attraction from sexual or romantic attraction. *(October 28, about 00:12–00:20.)*

## Slide 3 - Attractiveness and first impressions

**What the slide shows:** Portraits are paired with positive trait judgments such as being sociable, altruistic, or intelligent.

**Detailed notes:** The halo effect means a positive judgment in one domain can spill over into a different domain. People may infer positive personality qualities from appearance without direct evidence. Repeated favourable responses can also create more opportunities to practise social skills, so the social treatment and later behaviour are not independent. The lecture compared this opportunity mechanism to a tall child being invited to play basketball more often.

**Key takeaway:** A trait attribution and a trait itself are different; differential opportunities are a separate explanation from the initial inference. *(October 28, about 00:15–00:23.)*

## Slide 4 - Symmetry

**What the slide shows:** A face and a mirrored body diagram illustrate symmetry.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture considered why symmetry can be read as a cue to health or development. A cue is imperfect: the observer is making an inference, not measuring someone's health, and a proposed evolutionary explanation does not decide every individual preference.

**Key takeaway:** Separate perceived attractiveness, a possible cue, and the actual characteristic the cue is supposed to signal. *(October 28, about 00:23–00:30.)*

## Slide 5 - Averageness and familiarity

**What the slide shows:** A face without individual features accompanies a proposed familiarity explanation.

**Detailed notes:** Averageness in this context means proximity to a typical configuration, not being mediocre or receiving an average rating. A composite can smooth unusual features. The lecture used generated faces to illustrate how a system can easily produce a conventional-looking attractive face. Familiarity is one proposed reason typical-looking configurations are liked.

**Key takeaway:** Averageness is about the pattern of features; it is distinct from bilateral symmetry. *(October 28, about 00:30–00:34.)*

## Slide 6 - Other appearance cues

**What the slide shows:** Photographs prompt discussion of cues beyond facial shape.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture discussed features such as skin and hair as cues people may interpret. The importance attached to them is not fixed, and an appearance judgment does not establish an underlying condition or a person's worth.

**Key takeaway:** Appearance judgments use multiple cues and are not diagnoses. *(October 28, about 00:33–00:37.)*

## Slide 7 - Cultural and historical variation

**What the slide shows:** A clock and a crowd illustrate variation over time and across groups.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture contrasted changing ideals in fashion, grooming, and body presentation across eras. Some cues may recur while their relative weight changes. Test claims about universal preferences against variation rather than trying to explain every example with biology alone.

**Key takeaway:** An ideal can be widespread in one setting without being timeless or universal. *(October 28, about 00:36–00:44.)*

## Slide 8 - Reported partner preferences

**What the slide shows:** Gender symbols contrast aggregate preferences, alongside traits such as kindness and dependability that both groups value.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture examined reported average gender differences in emphasis on looks or status. It also discussed how changing economic roles and expectations may change those patterns. Average differences do not support predictions about a particular person, and a study's period and sample matter.

**Key takeaway:** Interpret group comparisons as conditional averages, not gender rules. *(October 28, about 00:44–00:53.)*

## Slide 9 - Competing interpretations of body cues

**What the slide shows:** A thinker considers body measurements and pregnancy in separate thought bubbles.

**Detailed notes:** The imagery prompts questions about reproductive interpretations of preferences. Treat these as proposed explanations, not as permission to infer an individual's reproductive capacity or desires from appearance. The lecture contrasted possible biological explanations with social and historical variation.

**Key takeaway:** A proposed explanation for a group-level pattern does not determine what an individual wants. *(October 28, about 00:42–00:53; overlapping discussion.)*

## Slide 10 - How preferences are studied

**What the slide shows:** Newspaper advertisements are contrasted with a dating interface.

**Detailed notes:** Personal ads, study volunteers, and dating-platform activity are different kinds of evidence. What people say they value may not match whom they approach. Platform data can be extensive without being representative: participation, interface design, and the available pool shape the choices observed.

**Key takeaway:** Ask who was measured and whether a study measures an answer, an action, or a relationship outcome. *(October 28, about 00:54–00:57.)*

## Slide 11 - Social rewards

**What the slide shows:** A list distinguishes pleasure, learning by association, perceived prestige, and social benefits.

**Detailed notes:** Being with someone judged attractive may be rewarding in different ways. One possibility is the pleasant experience of looking at them; another is the status or attention people expect by association. Neither mechanism demonstrates that attractiveness itself causes any particular personality trait.

**Key takeaway:** Distinguish the value an observer places on association from a claim about the other person's character. *(October 28, about 00:56–01:00.)*

## Slide 12 - Do opposites attract?

**What the slide shows:** Two profiles and layers of possible traits accompany the familiar question.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture distinguished memorable differences from underlying similarity. Partners who enjoy different hobbies may still share assumptions about what matters, what is fair, and how to organize their lives. A salient difference is not evidence that the relationship forms because they are opposites overall.

**Key takeaway:** Ask what kind of similarity or difference matters for the outcome, rather than counting all differences equally. *(October 28, about 00:59–01:04.)*

## Slide 13 - Dimensions of similarity

**What the slide shows:** Tiles show demographic, interpersonal, and value characteristics behind a person considering a match.

**Detailed notes:** Education, age, values, and sense of humour are not interchangeable. Large data sets can make it possible to compare trivial dimensions as well as consequential ones; ease of measurement is not a reason to treat a feature as important.

**Key takeaway:** Shared central values may carry more weight than superficial matches. *(October 28, about 01:03–01:08.)*

## Slide 14 - Why similarity can be reinforcing

**What the slide shows:** Two globe-shaped eyes represent shared outlooks.

**Detailed notes:** Agreement can make a person feel understood and make recurring joint decisions easier. Differences create possible points of negotiation, especially on central issues; they do not make a durable relationship impossible.

**Key takeaway:** Similarity can help because it validates a perspective and reduces friction, not because partners need to be identical. *(October 28, about 01:06–01:10.)*

## Slide 15 - Proximity

**What the slide shows:** A pair in silhouette accompanies the idea that daily settings influence whom one meets.

**Detailed notes:** People who share a class, workplace, or neighbourhood have more chances to interact. They may already share interests or circumstances that brought them into that setting. Proximity can therefore modify opportunity and also make the people one encounters a selected group.

**Key takeaway:** A tie that appears to reflect personal preference may partly reflect who was available to meet. *(October 28, closing minutes; November 2, opening minutes.)*

## Slide 16 - Mere exposure

**What the slide shows:** An audience provides an example of people seeing each other repeatedly.

**Detailed notes:** Repeated encounters can make an initially unfamiliar person more familiar and easier to approach. Work or school connections illustrate why someone can become likable without immediately finding an important shared characteristic. The effect is a tendency, not a guarantee that repeated negative encounters become pleasant.

**Key takeaway:** Proximity makes exposure possible; mere exposure describes a potential effect of familiarity on liking. *(November 2, first 7 minutes.)*

## Slide 17 - Affect and attraction

**What the slide shows:** Smiling figures illustrate how affect can signal approach or avoidance.

**Detailed notes:** The deck frames positive affect as a signal that an encounter is inviting and negative affect as a possible signal to withdraw. This is a different mechanism from simply recognizing a face. The specific extent of discussion of this image is uncertain in the recording.

**Key takeaway:** Familiarity and the feeling attached to an encounter are distinct. *(November 2 opening discussion; slide-specific attribution uncertain.)*

## Slide 18 - Companionate love

**What the slide shows:** An older couple illustrates a lasting relationship.

**Detailed notes:** Early passion can be strong and absorbing. Over time, friendship, respect, concern for welfare, and shared interests can become more central. A reduction in early intensity is not by itself evidence of weaker love; the mix of components can change.

**Key takeaway:** Long-term connection may have a different structure from early attraction rather than less value. *(November 2, about 00:07–00:13.)*

## Slide 19 - Closeness and intimacy

**What the slide shows:** Pairs of circles depict increasing overlap between self and other.

**Detailed notes:** Reciprocated disclosure, acceptance, and support are ways closeness can become meaningful. Overlap represents how much one includes the other person in one's understanding of oneself; it is not geographical distance or a requirement that partners lose independence. A geographically separated couple can still be important to each other.

**Key takeaway:** Intimacy here means psychological closeness and is not synonymous with sexual contact. *(November 2, about 00:13–00:17.)*

## Slide 20 - Communal relationships

**What the slide shows:** A scoreboard illustrates the contrast between tracking exchanges and responding to needs.

**Detailed notes:** In a communal pattern, partners offer support because the other person needs it, without demanding an equivalent return for each act. This does not require one person to give indefinitely with no support. Overall reciprocity or equity is different from keeping a running tally of specific favours.

**Key takeaway:** A relationship can be fair without every act being repaid immediately. *(November 2, about 00:17–00:20.)*

## Slide 21 - Interdependence and commitment

**What the slide shows:** A film strip represents accumulated shared experiences and a detailed mental representation of the other person.

**Detailed notes:** Commitment is about continuing the relationship and can require making choices that affect future opportunities. An important relationship may not support a large long-term commitment; choices about work, education, or location can make the difference visible. Shared responsibilities can also persist when a romantic relationship ends.

**Key takeaway:** Distinguish how important a relationship feels now from what people plan and invest for its future. *(November 2, about 00:20–00:29.)*

## Slide 22 - Sternberg's triangle of love

**What the slide shows:** Intimacy, passion, and commitment form the three vertices. Corners represent one component, edges two, and the centre all three.

**Detailed notes:** Intimacy alone is liking, passion alone infatuation, and commitment alone empty love. Intimacy plus passion is romantic love; intimacy plus commitment is companionate love; passion plus commitment is fatuous love. All three make consummate love. The lecture used hypothetical changes in a relationship to show how a combination can change over time. The names are labels within a model, not moral judgments or a universal sequence.

**Key takeaway:** Identify the components present or absent rather than matching a relationship's social label to a type. *(November 2, about 00:28–00:41.)*

## Slide 23 - Secure attachment

**What the slide shows:** A launch pad represents a secure base from which a child can explore.

**Detailed notes:** Responsive caregiving helps make exploration possible. In the separation-and-reunion example, the child may be distressed by separation but seeks comfort when the caregiver returns and can resume exploration. The useful evidence is the pattern across exploration, separation, and reunion, not whether a child ever cries.

**Key takeaway:** Secure attachment balances seeking comfort with confidence to explore. *(November 2, about 00:42–00:46.)*

## Slide 24 - Anxious-ambivalent attachment

**What the slide shows:** A child holding tightly to a caregiver illustrates uncertainty about closeness.

**Detailed notes:** In the lecture's setup, a child can strongly seek proximity yet remain distressed or difficult to soothe upon reunion. The pattern differs from a secure reunion, where comforting enables a return to exploration.

**Key takeaway:** Seeking closeness and being reassured by closeness are not the same observation. *(November 2, about 00:46–00:48.)*

## Slide 25 - Avoidant attachment

**What the slide shows:** An image of isolation accompanies a description of distance and difficulty expressing emotion.

**Detailed notes:** In the reunion example, a child may show little outward search for comfort or turn away. Low visible distress should not be read as proof of well-being or independence. Classroom discussion of group patterns and later outcomes does not make early behaviour a fixed destiny or a diagnostic label.

**Key takeaway:** Pay attention to the response to reunion, not simply how loudly distress is expressed. *(November 2, about 00:47–00:52.)*

## Slide 26 - Attachment and self/other concerns

**What the slide shows:** A two-by-two table crosses whether self-concern and other-concern goals are met. It sets out secure, avoidant, anxious-ambivalent, and fearful attachment.

**Detailed notes:** The table reframes the attachment discussion in the course's self/other language. Secure is in the cell where both kinds of concern are met. The instructor explicitly noted that fearful attachment was not developed in the lecture; the fourth cell is deck content, not an additional taught pattern.

**Key takeaway:** Use the table to organize the three developed patterns without assuming equal lecture coverage of all four cells. *(November 2, about 00:52.)*

## Slide 27 - Relationship maintenance and well-being

**What the slide shows:** A roadwork sign represents the work involved in maintaining relationships.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture discussed health and support in relation to marriage, and emphasized that a marriage is not the only source of sustained care. Networks of family, friends, and community can also meet support needs. The existence of a statistical association does not establish a particular person's experience.

**Key takeaway:** Identify the support mechanism rather than assuming a relationship label itself delivers the benefit. *(November 2, about 00:52–00:57.)*

## Slide 28 - Productive conflict resolution

**What the slide shows:** A graphic highlights conflict as something people can work through.

**Detailed notes:** Avoidance may remove immediate discomfort without addressing the underlying disagreement. Working through a manageable conflict can build skills relevant to relationships at home, at school, and at work. The discussion concerned productive responses, not a claim that harmful conflict is beneficial.

**Key takeaway:** Differentiate resolving a problem from simply not talking about it. *(November 2, about 00:57–00:59.)*

## Slide 29 - Realistic optimism

**What the slide shows:** Signs encourage a positive orientation toward a difficult situation.

**Detailed notes:** Believing a relationship matters and that both people can work on a problem supports trying to resolve it. This is not forced cheerfulness, denial, or a guarantee of success.

**Key takeaway:** Optimism is an expectation that constructive effort can help, not a substitute for that effort. *(November 2, about 00:59–01:00.)*

## Slide 30 - Fairness and perspective taking

**What the slide shows:** Stacked stones illustrate balance.

**Detailed notes:** Anger can make it hard to see beyond one's own interpretation. Fairness requires trying to identify both people’s unmet needs, not just establishing who is right. Understanding someone's perspective does not require agreeing with it.

**Key takeaway:** Consider both viewpoints before deciding what a fair resolution would address. *(November 2, about 01:00.)*

## Slide 31 - Showing care during disagreement

**What the slide shows:** A small gift-like object accompanies an invitation to do things that please a partner.

**Detailed notes:** It is possible to communicate care while still objecting to what happened. Treating the other person as important and naming a specific conflict are compatible; care does not mean abandoning a legitimate concern.

**Key takeaway:** Distinguish how you regard the person from how you assess the problem. *(November 2, about 01:00–01:01.)*

## Slide 32 - Enjoying shared activities

**What the slide shows:** A group shares a recreational cycling activity.

**Detailed notes:** A connection can be maintained through positive shared experiences, not just repaired through conflict discussions. The activity shown is illustrative, not a prescription; different people enjoy different activities.

**Key takeaway:** Maintenance also involves doing enjoyable things together. *(November 2, about 01:01.)*

## Slide 33 - Shared values and beliefs

**What the slide shows:** Value words radiate around silhouettes.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture returned to the earlier point about consequential similarities. Expressing what one appreciates about the other person and recognizing shared commitments can help put a disagreement into the larger context of the relationship.

**Key takeaway:** The quality and importance of what is shared matter more than the number of similarities. *(November 2, about 01:02.)*

## Slide 34 - Communication

**What the slide shows:** Overlapping hands suggest attentive connection and support.

**Detailed notes:** Difficult conversations are easy to postpone. Communication means addressing the disagreement and expressing needs rather than assuming that silence has resolved the problem. This complements perspective taking on Slide 30.

**Key takeaway:** A productive conversation makes each person's concerns available for discussion. *(November 2, about 01:02.)*

## Slide 35 - Sexual intimacy

**What the slide shows:** Two figures surround a heart and are composed of words about trust, listening, and connection.

**Detailed notes:** In a romantic relationship, sexual intimacy can express connection and care. It is not the definition of intimacy in Sternberg's triangle, and close ties can be deeply intimate without being sexual.

**Key takeaway:** Differentiate one way to express connection from the broader psychological component of intimacy. *(November 2, about 01:02–01:03.)*

## Slide 36 - Endings

**What the slide shows:** A closing curtain accompanies the point that relationships can end and separation can hurt.

**Detailed notes:** The slide closes the chapter by recognizing that not all relationships persist. The recording moves into helping and altruism shortly afterward. Do not infer a separate theory of breakups from the closing illustration.

**Key takeaway:** Maintaining a relationship takes work, but maintenance is not a guarantee of permanence. *(November 2, end of chapter; depth of slide-specific discussion uncertain.)*
