# Chapter 8: Helping and Altruism

*Draft lecture notes. Slide numbers follow the PDF. Recording times are approximate.*

## What this chapter is about

Helping can be motivated by other-concern, self-concern, or both. The chapter connects those motives to emotion, the decisions a bystander makes, and how the recipient experiences the resulting help.

**Key takeaway:** Use the following slides for the content.

**Detailed notes:** The chapter moves from motives to circumstances and then considers the recipient's point of view.

**Key takeaway:** Helping is neither just a trait nor just a response to an emergency.

## Slide 3 - Self and other concerns

**What the slide shows:** The first topic is highlighted in the chapter map.

**Detailed notes:** A helpful action can meet another person's needs while also serving the helper. The two kinds of concern need not be opposites.

**Key takeaway:** Separate the beneficiary of an act from its motive.

*Source: November 2, about 01:04–01:06.*

## Slide 4 - Why volunteer?

**What the slide shows:** A photograph accompanies two questions about volunteering.

**Detailed notes:** Possible motives include making a useful contribution, developing skills, and gaining recognition. More than one can coexist. Asking why someone acted is different from asking who benefited.

**Key takeaway:** The term volunteering does not identify a single motive.

*Source: November 2, about 01:05–01:08.*

## Slide 5 - Altruism and direct reward

**What the slide shows:** The definition emphasizes the recipient's welfare and absence of direct reward to the helper.

**Detailed notes:** Prosocial or helpful behaviour is the broader category. The lecture framed altruism around aiming to help without expecting a return. A public award after the fact does not show that it was the goal; expecting status in advance changes the inference. Feeling good afterward does not by itself establish the original motive.

**Key takeaway:** Ask what return the person anticipated, not just what happened afterward.

*Source: November 2, about 01:04–01:11.*

## Slide 6 - Examples of helping

**What the slide shows:** Blood donation and volunteering illustrate possible helpful acts.

**Detailed notes:** The same act can be done anonymously, for service credit, or for a mix of reasons. Compare consequences and motives rather than labelling the act by appearance. The lecture contrasted donating for recognition with anonymous giving.

**Key takeaway:** Evidence of benefit is not necessarily evidence of self-sacrifice or a specific motive.

*Source: November 2, about 01:06–01:11; November 4 opening.*

## Slide 7 - Kinship and fitness

**What the slide shows:** The diagram distinguishes an individual's own survival from the continuation of shared genes.

**Detailed notes:** An evolutionary account asks how helping relatives could indirectly support shared genetic interests even when helping is costly. It is an explanation of a possible selection pattern, not a claim that people calculate genetic payoffs or should favour relatives.

**Key takeaway:** Separate an evolutionary explanation from an immediate motive or a moral rule.

*Source: November 4, about 00:04–00:09.*

## Slide 8 - Closeness of relatives

**What the slide shows:** Close versus distant relatives are compared in an emergency example.

**Detailed notes:** Kinship predicts a greater inclination to incur costs for closer relatives, other factors being equal. Emotional closeness and prior interaction also differ in ordinary families, so an individual example cannot isolate the evolutionary account.

**Key takeaway:** A predicted tendency is not a guarantee; compare like circumstances.

*Source: November 4, about 00:06–00:09.*

## Slide 9 - Similarity and group membership

**What the slide shows:** Overlapping circles represent friends, in-group members, and strangers.

**Detailed notes:** Recognizing a bond or shared identity can change how willing someone is to help. Perceived similarity can be socially meaningful without demonstrating genetic relatedness.

**Key takeaway:** Differentiate actual kinship, perceived similarity, and common group membership.

*Source: November 4, about 00:08–00:11.*

## Slide 10 - Reciprocal altruism

**What the slide shows:** The two figures illustrate offering help now that may be returned later.

**Detailed notes:** When people expect to interact again, providing help can be sustained by the possibility of later return. It differs from the strict no-expected-return account on Slide 5 and need not involve an immediate trade.

**Key takeaway:** Identify the role of future reciprocity rather than only observing that a favour occurred.

*Source: November 4, about 00:10–00:14.*

## Slide 11 - When reciprocity sustains helping

**What the slide shows:** Two pairs illustrate exchanges over time.

**Detailed notes:** Repeated cooperation can leave both participants better off. A relationship in which one side never returns assistance raises a different question: willingness to help may decline when the pattern feels exploitative.

**Key takeaway:** The benefit depends on a pattern across encounters, not necessarily an exact exchange.

*Source: November 4, about 00:11–00:16.*

## Slide 12 - Reinforcement and social learning

**What the slide shows:** A child with an adult accompanies text about consequences and learning.

**Detailed notes:** People can learn from seeing someone help and from what happens afterward. Social approval or material rewards may maintain a helpful behaviour without proving that the behaviour was motivated by pure altruism.

**Key takeaway:** Separate the acquisition of a behaviour from why a person does it on a particular occasion.

*Source: November 4, about 00:16–00:21; slide-specific boundary approximate.*

## Slide 13 - Social responsibility norm

**What the slide shows:** People distributing supplies illustrate a shared expectation of helping.

**Detailed notes:** A norm can support assistance to people who need it even when they cannot repay it. The lecture discussed expectations in family and public settings; these may operate at an institutional as well as an interpersonal scale.

**Key takeaway:** Social responsibility is not the same reason for helping as expecting a future favour.

*Source: November 4, about 00:21–00:27.*

## Slide 14 - Helpful models in media

**What the slide shows:** A child watches a screen while the slide proposes a modelling effect.

**Detailed notes:** The deck extends observational learning to media: a viewer can see a helpful act and what follows it. The distinct media example is not clearly anchored to a separate transcript passage.

**Key takeaway:** Distinguish observing a model from receiving direct reinforcement. Do not treat the media example as confirmed test scope.

*Source: Slide-specific coverage uncertain.*

## Slide 15 - Review self and other concerns

**What the slide shows:** The slide lists objectives on altruism, costs, reciprocity, and norms.

**Detailed notes:** Review Slides 5, 7, 10, and 13 by comparing possible explanations for the same helpful act. Do not use these objectives to infer what will appear on the test.

**Key takeaway:** Compare explanations instead of labelling a person as simply helpful or selfish.

*Source: Deck review divider.*

## Slide 16 - Mood and emotions

**What the slide shows:** The second topic is highlighted in the chapter map.

**Detailed notes:** The next section asks how affect changes the willingness to help and why positive and negative emotions need separate explanations.

**Key takeaway:** Mood may change behaviour without settling the question of motive.

*Source: November 4, about 00:27–00:29.*

## Slide 17 - Mood and willingness to help

**What the slide shows:** Face icons pose the question of when people are more helpful.

**Detailed notes:** A positive mood can support helping, but a negative emotion can also sometimes prompt action. The outcome depends on what the situation means and what helping is expected to accomplish.

**Key takeaway:** Do not reduce helping to a rule that happy people help and unhappy people do not.

*Source: November 4, about 00:28–00:32.*

## Slide 18 - Positive mood

**What the slide shows:** A collage depicts happiness and positive connection.

**Detailed notes:** When things seem to be going well, approaching another person may seem easier, and helping may sustain a positive view of oneself. A good mood is neither a necessary nor sufficient condition for intervention.

**Key takeaway:** A mood effect is a tendency that depends on the opportunity to help.

*Source: November 4, about 00:28–00:33.*

## Slide 19 - How positive mood may promote helping

**What the slide shows:** Arrows lead through perceived safety, liking others, and feeling good about oneself.

**Detailed notes:** These are possible explanatory pathways. For example, the environment seeming safe can reduce hesitation even if the helper's opinion of the other person has not changed. The diagram does not show that all three paths are required.

**Key takeaway:** Identify the intermediate process that links mood and behaviour.

*Source: November 4, about 00:28–00:34.*

## Slide 20 - Negative emotions and helping

**What the slide shows:** A collage contrasts several unpleasant states.

**Detailed notes:** Unpleasant emotions sometimes motivate action, for example when unfairness is apparent or a person tries to make amends. Whether an emotion promotes helping depends on how it directs attention and what options are available.

**Key takeaway:** Describe the particular emotion and mechanism, not just its negative valence.

*Source: November 4, about 00:29–00:35.*

## Slide 21 - Guilt and symbolic repair

**What the slide shows:** A Macbeth image accompanies the idea of wanting to feel cleansed after doing wrong.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture discussed being extra helpful after doing something one regrets. If restoring one's own self-image is part of the aim, the act can still help someone while involving self-concern. The slide's metaphor is not evidence that helping necessarily removes guilt.

**Key takeaway:** Good consequences and guilt-reduction motives can coexist.

*Source: November 4, about 00:34–00:36.*

## Slide 22 - Sadness, anger, and fear

**What the slide shows:** Three photos distinguish examples of negative affect.

**Detailed notes:** Anger at injustice can motivate assistance or collective action; other unpleasant feelings may change what aid seems possible or worthwhile. These emotions do not have a uniform effect on helping.

**Key takeaway:** A relevant appraised situation matters more than whether an emotion is simply pleasant or unpleasant.

*Source: November 4, about 00:30–00:37, overlapping discussion.*

## Slide 23 - Personal distress

**What the slide shows:** An uncomfortable cartoon depicts feeling upset when witnessing suffering.

**Detailed notes:** The observer's own discomfort can motivate reducing that discomfort. Helping is one route; disengaging can be another. To infer the motive, consider how someone responds when leaving is easy.

**Key takeaway:** The object of concern is the observer's aversive state, even though someone else's suffering prompted it.

*Source: November 4, about 00:36–00:39.*

## Slide 24 - Empathy

**What the slide shows:** Joined hands illustrate understanding and responding to another's distress.

**Detailed notes:** Empathy involves trying to understand and feel what another person is experiencing, rather than simply noticing one's own unpleasant response. The same outward help could result from either empathy or personal distress; behaviour alone does not decide.

**Key takeaway:** Compare whether the focus is the other person's welfare or relief of one's own discomfort.

*Source: November 4, about 00:37–00:39.*

## Slide 25 - Review mood and emotion

**What the slide shows:** The slide summarizes affect-related goals.

**Detailed notes:** Compare someone offering help in a good mood, after feeling guilty, or because they understand another person's need. The act may look identical while the explanation differs.

**Key takeaway:** Explain the mechanism and say what evidence would distinguish it.

*Source: Deck review divider.*

## Slide 26 - Social context

**What the slide shows:** The third circle in the chapter map is highlighted.

**Detailed notes:** Bystander intervention depends on a sequence of judgments, not just a stable willingness to help.

**Key takeaway:** Locate where a situation blocks help.

*Source: November 4, about 00:39–00:40.*

## Slide 27 - Latané and Darley's helping model

**What the slide shows:** Arrows lead from noticing an event to interpreting it, assuming responsibility, identifying appropriate assistance, and intervening; each preceding stage can lead to no help.

**Detailed notes:** The model diagnoses different explanations for the same outcome. Someone who misses the event has a different barrier from someone who sees it, recognizes the need, but thinks another person will act.

**Key takeaway:** Find the earliest decision point at which evidence shows progress stopped.

*Source: November 4, about 00:39–00:41.*

## Slide 28 - Notice the event

**What the slide shows:** An observer reacts to an unusual scene.

**Detailed notes:** Noticing requires attention. Before assigning a motive or evaluating an interpretation, establish that the observer picked up the relevant cue.

**Key takeaway:** Not noticing is not deciding that nothing should be done.

*Source: November 4, about 00:40–00:42.*

## Slide 29 - Distraction

**What the slide shows:** A busy street accompanies arrows from distraction to missed detection.

**Detailed notes:** A crowded, distracting environment can divert attention. The lecture also discussed public norms of keeping to oneself. If someone saw and understood the event, distraction alone does not explain why they did not act.

**Key takeaway:** Separate attentional load from later judgments.

*Source: November 4, about 00:41–00:44.*

## Slide 30 - Interpret the event

**What the slide shows:** The same observer now construes the unusual scene as an emergency.

**Detailed notes:** An event can be visible but ambiguous. Context may make observers interpret apparent danger as acting, play, or an expected feature of an event.

**Key takeaway:** Seeing a cue and recognizing the need for help are separate stages.

*Source: November 4, about 00:44–00:46.*

## Slide 31 - Pluralistic ignorance

**What the slide shows:** The observer changes their reading of the scene after other people treat it as a movie set.

**Detailed notes:** In ambiguity, bystanders use others' behaviour as information. Multiple people can each appear calm while privately uncertain; a false impression of agreement can make actual need less apparent.

**Key takeaway:** The question here is whether the situation is an emergency, not whose job it is to help.

*Source: November 4, about 00:45–00:48.*

## Slide 32 - Assume responsibility

**What the slide shows:** An observer concludes that they personally need to act.

**Detailed notes:** An observer may identify a need accurately yet fail to see themselves as the person responsible for a response. This is different from confusing the event with something harmless.

**Key takeaway:** Understanding that someone should act is not yet accepting that you should act.

*Source: November 4, about 00:47–00:49.*

## Slide 33 - Diffusion of responsibility

**What the slide shows:** A person in a crowd imagines that another observer will take action.

**Detailed notes:** Responsibility can feel spread across the group. The lecture contrasted an open appeal to a crowd with naming a person and a concrete task. The latter reduces uncertainty about who is responsible.

**Key takeaway:** Use diffusion when the obstacle is who should act, not whether help is needed.

*Source: November 4, about 00:48–00:54.*

## Slide 34 - Know appropriate assistance

**What the slide shows:** Mannequins illustrate having an action in mind.

**Detailed notes:** Recognizing and accepting responsibility do not guarantee knowing how to respond. Asking for qualified assistance is different from attempting an unfamiliar response. The slide illustrates a decision stage, not a first-aid procedure.

**Key takeaway:** Knowledge and readiness can remove a barrier after the need is recognized.

*Source: November 4, about 00:50–00:56.*

## Slide 35 - Skill, confidence, and intervention

**What the slide shows:** An uncertain observer is contrasted with a trained responder.

**Detailed notes:** Training can change whether a person knows a feasible action. Costs or safety concerns may remain even after someone knows a response. The lecture considered different ways to obtain assistance without assuming everyone should intervene directly.

**Key takeaway:** Knowing how to help and deciding whether direct help is safe are different judgments.

*Source: November 4, about 00:50–01:01.*

## Slide 36 - Review the stages

**What the slide shows:** A review objective asks how social variables affect each stage.

**Detailed notes:** Trace an example in order, from notice through action. For instance, a distracted observer and an observer who assumes someone else has called may both fail to help for different reasons.

**Key takeaway:** Match evidence to a specific bottleneck.

*Source: Deck review divider.*

## Slide 37 - Other determinants

**What the slide shows:** The fourth chapter topic is highlighted.

**Detailed notes:** The final section considers person, role, culture, and how help is received.

**Key takeaway:** A useful account may require both helper and recipient perspectives.

*Source: November 4, about 01:01–01:03.*

## Slide 38 - Person, gender, and cultural variables

**What the slide shows:** A collage separates three families of possible influence.

**Detailed notes:** A person's habitual behaviour, role expectations, and the situation can each change what helping looks like. Comparing only one kind of helping can miss other forms of support.

**Key takeaway:** Consider which opportunities and behaviours were measured.

*Source: November 4, about 01:01–01:09.*

## Slide 39 - Prosocial personality

**What the slide shows:** A volunteering image accompanies a definition based on patterns across situations.

**Detailed notes:** Someone may consistently respond more helpfully across settings. One generous or unhelpful action is weak evidence of a trait, and prosocial patterns can be learned and reinforced.

**Key takeaway:** A trait claim needs repeated patterns, not one example.

*Source: November 4, about 01:01–01:04.*

## Slide 40 - Overall helpfulness

**What the slide shows:** An equality sign separates two gender silhouettes.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture distinguished aggregate helpfulness from which kind of help tends to be expressed or expected. A contrast in one setting does not establish a difference in general helpfulness.

**Key takeaway:** Define the outcome before interpreting an apparent group difference.

*Source: November 4, about 01:05.*

## Slide 41 - Situational differences

**What the slide shows:** Photos contrast caregiving, public rescue, and roles presented as heroic.

**Detailed notes:** Social expectations may make different actions more visible or encouraged in different settings. Do not infer capabilities or motives from a person's gender, and do not equate physical intervention with helping in general.

**Key takeaway:** Availability, norms, and the kind of opportunity can explain different patterns.

*Source: November 4, about 01:05–01:09.*

## Slide 42 - Just-world beliefs and deservingness

**What the slide shows:** Speech bubbles contrast dispositional and situational accounts of homelessness.

**Detailed notes:** Believing people get what they deserve can change whether observers view them as deserving of assistance. The political contrast is schematic, not a statement about every group member. Link this to earlier attribution material: the same need can be interpreted as a result of circumstances or personal choices.

**Key takeaway:** A deservingness judgment is a perception, not evidence about the causes of the hardship.

*Source: November 4, about 01:09–01:10.*

## Slide 43 - Dependency-oriented help

**What the slide shows:** A would-be rescuer and a vulnerable figure illustrate taking over.

**Detailed notes:** Providing a solution for someone can be useful in some situations but may communicate that they are incapable or leave them unable to handle the next instance. The lecture contrasted completing a task for someone with helping them learn it.

**Key takeaway:** Assess what the help does to future capacity and how the recipient may interpret it.

*Source: November 4, about 01:10–01:11.*

## Slide 44 - Autonomy-oriented help

**What the slide shows:** A person supports another person's climb, rather than replacing their effort.

**Detailed notes:** Tools, instruction, or support can allow a person to make their own decisions and handle future tasks. Decide which form of help fits the problem rather than assuming one is always appropriate.

**Key takeaway:** Autonomy-oriented help supports the recipient's ability to act.

*Source: November 4, about 01:11.*

## Slide 45 - Review other determinants

**What the slide shows:** Learning objectives ask about the helper, the recipient, and ways to increase help.

**Detailed notes:** Apply both perspectives: an action can lower the helper's uncertainty but still be unwanted or undermining to the receiver. Use the prior slides to explain what change would remove which barrier.

**Key takeaway:** More help is not always better help; ask what the person needs and wants.

*Source: Deck review divider.*
