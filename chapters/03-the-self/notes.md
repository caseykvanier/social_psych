# Chapter 3: The Self

## What this chapter is about

The self is not treated as an isolated inner object. It is a cognitive representation that develops over time and is expressed in relation to social settings, roles, groups, and other people. The chapter separates the cognitive self, the feeling self, and the social self. The September 9 lecture covers the cognitive self through deindividuation. The September 16 recording reviews social comparison and completes social identity, self-presentation and self-monitoring. The September 14 lecture develops self-discrepancy, self-affirmation, self-esteem, narcissism, the looking-glass self, labelling, and social comparison.

## Slide 3 - Self-concept

### What the slide shows

The slide defines self-concept as knowledge about personality traits, physical characteristics, abilities, values, roles, and existence as an individual. A nested-circle diagram shows two overlapping versions of self: a focused, confident core and a broader self that adds judgment, distraction, criticism, and past- or future-oriented thought.

### Detailed notes

The instructor defines self-concept as the answer to "Who am I?" The answer is not necessarily one fixed personality. Sleeping, intoxicated, school, work, home, friendship, religious, and formal-authority contexts can make different actions, beliefs, and roles more accessible. These are not wholly separate people, but different configurations of the same person.

The nested circles show that versions of self can overlap. Some qualities may remain stable across contexts, while others appear only in particular settings. A mature self-concept can include apparently conflicting descriptions, such as being outgoing with close friends but quiet when feeling judged.

### Examples and applications

- "School you," "work you," and "friend you" can behave differently without any one version being false.
- A simple trait label such as "intelligent" becomes more informative when a person recognizes where and how their abilities are expressed.
- Development brings greater nuance: a young child may describe concrete preferences and relationships, whereas an older person can describe conditional traits and roles.

### Key takeaway

Self-concept is an organized but context-sensitive body of knowledge about who we are.

## Slide 4 - Mirror self-recognition

### What the slide shows

A collage shows people, a dog, a baby, and a monkey interacting with mirrors. The images introduce mirror self-recognition as evidence that an organism represents itself as distinct from the surrounding world.

### Detailed notes

The lecture describes the classic mark or "red dot" procedure. A younger child may see a marked face in the mirror and touch the reflection, treating it as another child. At a later developmental stage, the child touches their own face because they understand that the reflection corresponds to their body. This change indicates a developing representation of the self as an agent moving through the world.

The instructor uses animal comparisons to make the concept less obvious. Some primates and other cognitively sophisticated animals can show mirror self-recognition, while many animals respond as though the reflection were another animal. The broader point is that the apparently simple belief "that reflection is me" depends on a developed self-concept.

### Key takeaway

Recognizing oneself in a mirror reflects a developed distinction between self and environment.

## Slide 6 - Cultural differences in desire for uniqueness

### What the slide shows

A bar chart compares the percentage choosing an uncommon pen among East Asian and European American participants. European American participants choose the uncommon colour much more often in both pen distributions, while East Asian participants do so less often.

### Detailed notes

The instructor returns to this pen-choice study while discussing how the social context influences versions of self. Individualistic cultural settings more strongly reward standing out and presenting a consistent personal identity: "This is me; I am not changing for the group." Collectivistic settings place greater value on adapting to the group and fitting its demands. The instructor initially reverses this comparison while speaking, immediately corrects it, and gives the individualistic-versus-collectivistic interpretation above.

The graph specifically measures choice of an uncommon pen, so it is evidence about a behavioural expression of uniqueness. It should not be treated as proof that every member of either broad cultural category has the same self-concept.

### Key takeaway

Cultural norms can influence whether standing out or fitting in is treated as the more desirable expression of self.

## Slide 7 - Self-complexity

### What the slide shows

The slide defines self-complexity as having multiple, relatively independent ways of thinking about oneself. A cartoon depicts one person occupying several sharply different social roles.

### Detailed notes

Self-complexity includes both the number of selves and how differentiated they are. Imagine circles representing work, school, friendship, family, religious, and party selves. Highly differentiated circles overlap little; highly integrated circles overlap substantially.

A highly differentiated person may adapt very effectively to each setting. Their party self can fit a party perfectly and their family or religious self can fit those contexts equally well. The cost appears when social worlds collide: people from one context may be surprised by behaviour shown in another. A highly integrated person behaves more consistently and worries less about contexts colliding, but may be less perfectly adapted to any particular group.

Neither pattern is automatically correct. Differentiation can support flexibility; integration can support consistency. Even people who value "always being myself" are influenced by social situations more than they may recognize.

### Key takeaway

Self-complexity describes how many context-specific selves a person has and how much those selves overlap.

## Slide 8 - Self-concept clarity

### What the slide shows

The slide defines self-concept clarity as the extent to which a person's self-concept is clearly and consistently defined. The Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde image represents sharply conflicting identities.

### Detailed notes

Self-concept clarity tends to develop through experience. Childhood descriptions are often concrete and simple. Adolescence brings identity exploration: people try different roles, groups, values, and possible futures to discover which versions of self feel sustainable. With age, choices and their consequences often produce a more stable answer to "Who am I?"

Clarity has practical consequences because long-term decisions assume continuity between present and future selves. Educational debt, career plans, relationships, and parenthood all involve acting now for a person one expects to become. Greater clarity can support consistent planning. Low clarity can make costly commitments risky if the underlying goal is still being explored.

The instructor uses James Marcia's identity framework. Exploration or moratorium involves trying possibilities without full commitment. Foreclosure involves accepting an identity supplied by family or community without much exploration. Foreclosure is not automatically unhappy or wrong; it may fit a community where roles are stable and collectively defined. Conflict can arise when family expectations and an individual's preferred identity diverge.

### Examples and applications

- Friends and settings can bring out versions of oneself that feel better or worse; these reactions provide information during identity exploration.
- A commitment to law school makes more sense when the career fits a clear and tested self-concept than when it is an unexamined possibility.
- A person may inherit a family occupation without exploring alternatives and still build a satisfying life.

### Key takeaway

Self-concept clarity supports stable planning, but healthy clarity may emerge through exploration rather than premature commitment.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-09, approximately 00:53-01:03._

## Slide 9 - Self-consciousness

### What the slide shows

The slide defines self-consciousness as the heightened accessibility of self-concept when a person is concerned about being observed and judged. The photograph shows someone hiding their face.

### Detailed notes

Attention directed at the self makes beliefs about other people's judgments cognitively accessible. A person begins monitoring the gap between the public self they want to display and aspects they hope others will not notice. This can trigger negative self-talk and the assumption that observers are judging much more harshly than they actually are.

The lecture uses public speaking to show the asymmetry. A struggling presenter may think their failure proves that they are unintelligent, unlikeable, or incapable. Observers are unlikely to make those sweeping judgments; they usually see a person having a difficult presentation. The person under scrutiny nevertheless treats their own feared interpretation as if it were the audience's view.

Performance pressure can then become self-fulfilling. An athlete or presenter who worries intensely about failure may devote attention to monitoring the self rather than executing a well-practised task. Repeated experience can reduce this pressure. The instructor contrasts the self-consciousness of beginning teachers with the greater ease that comes after years of teaching.

### Key takeaway

Self-consciousness shifts attention toward imagined evaluation, often making other people's judgments seem harsher and more central than they are.

## Slide 10 - Deindividuation

### What the slide shows

The slide defines deindividuation as loss of individual self-awareness and accountability in groups. Images of uniformed or masked groups and alcohol use illustrate anonymity, group identity, and intoxication as possible routes to deindividuation.

### Detailed notes

Deindividuation blurs the boundary between "me" and the group. Anonymity reduces personal accountability; uniforms make individuals appear interchangeable; group membership supplies a shared identity; intoxication reduces anxiety and self-monitoring. These mechanisms can permit behaviour that a person would be less likely to perform alone and visibly identified.

The instructor compares masked Klan members, uniformed Nazis, masked immigration-enforcement officers discussed in relation to Minneapolis protests, alcohol use, and hockey riots. These are lecture examples used to isolate mechanisms, not claims that the cases are morally or historically equivalent. Masks can separate a hidden group self from the person's everyday public identity. Uniforms can foreground the organization rather than the individual. Alcohol both changes behaviour pharmacologically and provides a social explanation that can reduce perceived personal responsibility.

Large crowds can also produce emergent behaviour. An individual fan may never decide alone to damage a shop after a game, but anonymity, excitement, alcohol, and perceived group action can combine into a collective wave that differs from the intentions of its individual members.

### Key takeaway

Anonymity, reduced self-monitoring, and strong group identity can weaken individual accountability and make collective behaviour possible that members might reject alone.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-09, approximately 01:10-01:14:30._

## Slide 11 - Self-discrepancy and cognitive dissonance

### What the slide shows

Self-discrepancy theory concerns distress caused by a gap between actual and ideal selves. Cognitive dissonance is discomfort associated with inconsistency. A diagram shows inconsistency producing anxiety and ways to reduce it: changing an element or its importance, or adding a consonant belief that buffers the inconsistency.

### Detailed notes

The **actual self** is how a person understands their current behaviour and characteristics. The **ideal self** represents who they wish to be or the standards they want to meet. A **self-discrepancy** occurs when these representations do not match. The lecture also mentions a feared self: the person one worries about becoming. These imagined versions of oneself can give behaviour a direction.

Simply acting differently in two settings is not necessarily a distressing discrepancy. Being quiet in class and talkative with friends may be a comfortable adaptation to different roles. The important issue here is a perceived failure to meet a personally meaningful standard: "I want to be a responsible student, but I keep avoiding studying."

**Cognitive dissonance** is the uncomfortable tension associated with inconsistent beliefs, attitudes, or behaviour. The lecture concentrates on inconsistency between behaviour and a valued self-image. A self-discrepancy can therefore produce dissonance, but the terms identify different parts of the explanation: the mismatch and the discomfort associated with it.

### Examples and applications

A student who values academic success but repeatedly puts studying aside can reduce the tension in several ways:

- **Change behaviour:** set aside study time and remove distractions, bringing behaviour closer to the valued standard.
- **Change an attitude or reduce its importance:** decide that school does not matter, making the same behaviour less inconsistent with the revised standard.
- **Add a reassuring belief:** focus on being a helpful volunteer or a valued friend, protecting a broader sense of worth despite the academic problem.
- **Distract oneself:** turn to another activity to avoid dwelling on the discomfort. This can provide temporary relief without resolving the original mismatch.

These responses are not equally useful for the student's longer-term goals. Reducing discomfort does not necessarily mean improving the underlying situation. A rationalization can make someone feel better while leaving the behaviour unchanged.

The instructor connects this motivation with **negative reinforcement**: studying may become more likely when it removes an unpleasant feeling. Here "negative" refers to removing something aversive, not punishment or a morally bad action. The example illustrates one possible reason for studying, rather than claiming that all studying is guilt-driven.

### Key takeaway

A gap between behaviour and a valued self-image can motivate change, but people may change their behaviour, reinterpret their standards, or protect themselves from the discomfort in other ways.

## Slide 12 - Self-affirmation theory

### What the slide shows

Self-affirmation theory proposes that people can reduce a threat in one domain by focusing on their worth in another, sometimes unrelated, domain. A silhouette filled with positive qualities represents alternative sources of self-worth.

### Detailed notes

**Self-affirmation** protects a broader sense of personal worth when one aspect of the self is threatened. After a disappointing test, a person might remind themselves that they are a committed dancer, a reliable friend, or a helpful community member. The other valued domain gives the person a basis for feeling worthwhile beyond this one result.

The distinction is between reducing a threat to overall self-worth and solving the original problem. Feeling proud of dancing does not change the test grade or remove the need to study. Nevertheless, a person need not treat a single academic failure as evidence that their whole self is worthless.

The importance of the threatened domain matters. A beginner who does not care much about skateboarding may be less shaken by a poor attempt than someone who defines themselves through that skill. The same criticism can therefore have different consequences depending on how central that domain is to the person's identity.

Self-affirmation is not simply insisting that one is excellent at the very activity that went badly. The lecture's central example shifts attention to a different, genuinely valued source of worth.

### Key takeaway

Affirming another valued aspect of the self can buffer a threat to overall self-worth without directly fixing the problem that triggered it.

## Slide 14 - Self-esteem

### What the slide shows

Self-esteem is defined as positive or negative feelings about oneself. The photograph shows a storefront labelled "Self Esteem Shop."

### Detailed notes

**Self-esteem** is the evaluative side of the self: how positively or negatively a person feels about themselves. It differs from self-concept, which concerns the content of what a person believes about themselves. Knowing that one is a student is part of self-concept; feeling proud or disappointed about oneself as a student concerns self-esteem.

Self-esteem can draw on several domains, including academic performance, friendships, community standing, work, and appearance. People give these domains different weights. A disappointing result in a central domain may matter much more than poor performance at an activity the person does not value. Overall self-esteem therefore depends on both the person's evaluation of their performance and the importance attached to that domain.

The evaluation is subjective. A highly accomplished athlete can feel inadequate when comparing themselves with a few stronger competitors. Another person may feel proud of a modest skill. Objective achievement alone does not tell us exactly how someone feels about themselves.

### Correlation, causation, and feedback

The lecture uses associations between self-esteem and desirable outcomes to revisit **correlation versus causation**. If students with higher self-esteem tend to have better grades, that association alone cannot show that increasing self-esteem will improve grades. Success could improve self-esteem; confidence could influence effort or anxiety; other factors could contribute to both. These possibilities require evidence beyond the correlation.

The instructor contrasts credible, specific feedback with generic praise. A compliment from someone whose judgment matters, grounded in something the recipient actually did, may be meaningful. Reassurance that seems empty or undeserved may not be believed. The teaching point is that telling someone they are excellent is not equivalent to demonstrating a durable improvement in either self-esteem or performance.

### Development and comparison

The lecture contrasts a young child's pride in newly acquired abilities with the increasingly complex comparisons people make as they grow older. An older student may consider both their own progress and how they perform relative to peers. This is an illustration of changing reference points, not a claim that every child has high self-esteem or that all children develop identically.

A relative academic ranking can provide feedback, but it is not a measure of a person's overall worth. The chapter later develops how the choice of comparison group changes self-evaluation.

### Key takeaway

Self-esteem reflects subjective evaluations in personally important domains; neither objective success nor generic praise determines it automatically, and correlations do not establish causation.

## Slide 15 - Narcissism

### What the slide shows

The slide describes narcissism as a personality trait involving excessive self-admiration and self-centredness, using the shorthand of overly high self-esteem. The accompanying "selfie syndrome" image illustrates intense self-focus.

### Detailed notes

The lecture emphasizes a pattern of exaggerated self-importance, self-admiration, and difficulty accepting information that challenges a superior self-image. **Narcissism is not interchangeable with healthy confidence or simply feeling good about oneself.** The important contrast is whether the person can acknowledge limitations, consider others, and respond constructively to feedback.

A hypothetical student who insists they are exceptionally capable while dismissing every disappointing result as someone else's fault may protect their self-image without addressing ineffective study habits. If acknowledging a problem feels unacceptable, the motivation to improve may be undermined. The example connects narcissistic self-protection with the earlier discussion of dissonance reduction.

Persistent self-centredness can also strain relationships when other people's needs and perspectives receive little attention. The lecture distinguishes everyday trait language from a clinical disorder. These classroom concepts and examples do not establish a diagnosis of a public figure, classmate, or anyone else. The cause of narcissism was raised as a question without a definitive explanation being established in the lecture.

### Key takeaway

Exaggerated self-importance and defensive rejection of feedback differ from a positive but realistic self-evaluation that leaves room for learning and concern for others.

## Slide 17 - Looking-glass self

### What the slide shows

A person faces four mirrors representing imagined views held by parents, a girlfriend, an older brother, and an ex-girlfriend. The reflections differ, illustrating that perceived judgments vary across relationships.

### Detailed notes

The **looking-glass self** is the part of self-understanding shaped by how we believe other people see us. It involves our interpretation of their view, rather than direct access to what they actually think. A person can carry a mental model of another person's mental model of them.

Different relationships reflect different aspects of the self. A family member may express pride, a new friend may seem especially admiring, and someone involved in a past conflict may seem disapproving. These perceived views can affect self-esteem, behaviour, and which relationships or environments a person seeks out.

Neither praise nor criticism is automatically accurate. Others have incomplete information, and our interpretation of their reactions can also be mistaken. An outside perspective may reveal something useful, but it can also be unfair. The concept explains a social influence on self-understanding; it does not say that other people are the final authority on who someone is.

The lecture connects this process to prejudice. Repeated unfair treatment can affect how someone understands their place in the world, even when the judgment is undeserved. This leads to the next slide's distinction between external prejudice and adopting that prejudice into one's own self-concept.

### Key takeaway

Self-understanding is influenced by perceived social reflections, which can differ across relationships and need not be accurate or deserved.

## Slide 18 - Self-labelling and internalized prejudice

### What the slide shows

Self-labelling occurs when other people's labels become part of one's self-concept. Internalized prejudice occurs when prejudice directed at a person is adopted and turned toward the self. A wheelchair-accessibility symbol illustrates a socially salient category label; disability itself is not presented as a negative quality.

### Detailed notes

**Self-labelling** occurs when a label becomes a lens through which a person understands their own abilities, behaviour, or possibilities. Labels can be helpful when they make an experience understandable or support access to appropriate assistance. They can also constrain expectations when a person or their surroundings treats the category as defining everything they can do.

For example, repeatedly assuming that someone cannot perform a task may lead them to doubt a capacity they actually have. Conversely, recognizing a difficulty can replace a moralizing description such as "not trying" with a more useful understanding. The lecture uses diagnostic labels to illustrate this social process; it does not provide instructions for diagnosing or treating a condition, and a label does not determine every individual's capacities.

**Internalized prejudice** is more specific: an unfair belief about a group is incorporated into a member's beliefs about themselves. External discrimination and internalized prejudice are related but distinct. Being treated unfairly does not mean that the person necessarily accepts the prejudice.

### Examples and distinctions

The lecture discusses how gendered expectations can make activities seem "for me" or "not for me," influencing what someone is willing to try. Anticipating an unwelcome response may also discourage participation. Editorial clarification: avoidance by itself does not prove internalized prejudice: a person may recognize a real risk of unfair treatment without believing the negative stereotype.

An example involving a negative stereotype about women and mathematics illustrates how a testing context can make a stereotype salient and affect performance. The stereotype is not a fact about mathematical ability. Editorial clarification: distinguish the immediate pressure of a salient stereotype from personally endorsing it: a performance effect alone does not show that a participant believes the stereotype. The lecture links these social influences, but they should not be treated as identical mechanisms.

Other people's feedback can be informative without being correct. Their assumptions may overlook an individual's circumstances and strengths. A useful question is whether a label helps describe a specific need or has become an unsupported limit on the person's identity and opportunities.

### Key takeaway

Labels and prejudice can shape self-understanding and choices, but their effects vary; external treatment, accepting a label, and internalizing an unfair group belief are distinct processes.

## Slide 19 - Social comparison

### What the slide shows

The slide defines social comparison as learning about abilities, opinions, and relative social status by comparing oneself with others. Arrows distinguish upward, downward, and lateral comparisons.

### Detailed notes

The September 14 lecture introduces this topic, and the September 16 lecture begins by reviewing it. An **upward comparison** uses someone perceived as more skilled or successful as a reference point. That person may provide a model to emulate. A **downward comparison** uses someone less skilled or less experienced and can reveal how far one has progressed. A **lateral comparison** uses a peer group perceived as broadly similar and helps a person judge whether they are ahead of or behind that group.

The comparison group changes the meaning of the same performance. A student might feel capable in one class but behind in a more selective class, without their own skill having declined. Feeling behind can encourage effort when improvement seems attainable, but a very large gap can be discouraging. Conversely, a group that offers little challenge may make someone feel competent without motivating further development. Neither upward nor downward comparison has one guaranteed emotional effect.

The instructor compares this with video-game difficulty. A very easy setting can become uninteresting, while a setting that defeats the player immediately can feel hopeless. A challenge that is demanding but within reach can sustain effort. The analogy illustrates how the reference group affects motivation; it does not establish a universal ideal group for everyone.

### Key takeaway

Self-evaluation depends partly on whom we compare ourselves with. The same level of performance can feel different, and motivate different responses, in different peer groups.

## Slide 20 - Social identity theory

### What the slide shows

Social identity theory proposes that people draw part of their identity and self-esteem from social groups to which they belong. A large crowd at a sporting event illustrates shared emotion associated with group membership.

### Detailed notes

A self-concept includes more than personal accomplishments. Membership in a team, community, religious group, school, or other group can become part of the answer to "Who am I?" Events affecting that group can therefore affect how a member feels about themselves.

Sports fandom makes the distinction clear. A spectator may feel proud when a team wins and disappointed when it loses even though the spectator did not produce the result. The psychological connection to the team explains the emotional investment; it does not mean the fan's clothing or rituals caused the outcome.

People differ in the importance they give a group identity. A strongly identified fan may remain equally committed through repeated losses and also feel those losses intensely. Others make membership more or less prominent as the group's fortunes change. The lecture treats these as differing patterns, not as a rule that everyone abandons an unsuccessful group.

A person can also belong to groups whose claims compete. Regional and national identities, for example, can both matter while becoming difficult to reconcile in a particular discussion. Different situations make different memberships prominent. Having several social identities does not mean that only one is genuine.

### Key takeaway

Group memberships contribute to identity and self-esteem, so group outcomes and conflicts can be experienced as personally important.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-16, approximately within 00:00-00:15._

## Slide 21 - Basking in reflected glory

### What the slide shows

The slide defines basking in reflected glory as using and advertising an ingroup's positive achievements to boost self-esteem. An Arsenal football shirt illustrates making team membership visible.

### Detailed notes

**Basking in reflected glory** is the more specific process of emphasizing a successful group's connection to oneself. The achievement belongs to the group, but a member draws a positive feeling from the association and may display it publicly.

The lecture's example is wearing a team shirt after a championship win. A fan who did not play in the match may nevertheless talk and act as though the victory reflects on "us." After a loss, some fans instead make that membership less prominent and focus on another valued group, friendship, or activity. These shifts can help protect self-esteem.

This is related to social identity, but the terms do not mean exactly the same thing. Social identity concerns the broader place of membership in one's self-concept. Basking in reflected glory concerns highlighting a group's success as a positive reflection on oneself. The continued loyalty of some fans during poor seasons shows why membership should not be reduced to this one strategy.

### Key takeaway

People can highlight a successful ingroup to feel and appear positively connected to its achievement, even without having caused that achievement themselves.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-16, approximately within 00:00-00:15._

## Slide 22 - Self-presentation

### What the slide shows

The slide defines self-presentation as presenting a positive self-image to others with the goal of increasing social status. One photograph shows a conspicuous orange sports car; another shows people giving money to someone asking for help on the street.

### Detailed notes

**Self-presentation** involves trying to influence the attributes other people associate with us. Possessions, appearance, actions, and the setting in which others observe us all provide material for that impression. The image being communicated may be selective and need not establish what the person is actually like.

In the car example, a conspicuous sports car can communicate wealth, success, or a desire to be noticed. The teaching point is the public signal, not proof of the driver's wealth or motives. An action can also present moral qualities. Giving help where another person can see it may communicate generosity and produce social approval as well as helping the recipient.

The instructor uses a date as an illustrative setting in which someone may be especially motivated to appear generous. This is an example of audience-sensitive impression management, not a reported study establishing who gives most often or a claim that all helping is insincere. A helpful act can serve more than one purpose. The public interaction also raises questions about social position and how the person requesting help is perceived; the photograph alone cannot establish any participant's intentions.

These examples connect self-presentation with fitting into groups and gaining social recognition. The instructor informally connects the term with trying to create an "aura": a recognizable impression of who one is. The psychological concept concerns the behaviour and intended audience impression rather than the slang label.

### Key takeaway

People use visible actions and symbols to shape others' impressions; the audience and situation influence which qualities they try to display.

## Slide 23 - Self-monitoring and behavioural mimicry

### What the slide shows

The slide defines self-monitoring as motivation to regulate behaviour to meet social-situation demands. A bar chart compares face touching by participants assigned a worker or leader role. High self-monitors show approximately .12 in the worker role and .06 in the leader role; low self-monitors show approximately .07 and .08, respectively. Thus, the high-self-monitor group shows the larger difference across roles. These are approximate readings of the graph, not additional lecture data.

### Detailed notes

**Self-monitoring** concerns attention to how others perceive us and adjustment of behaviour to fit what the situation seems to require. High self-monitors are more sensitive to their audience and to the impression they are creating. Low self-monitors regulate their behaviour less around that audience's expectations. Lower self-monitoring does not guarantee either a good or a bad impression.

The lecture distinguishes a relatively stable tendency from situational change. Some people generally monitor themselves more than others, but circumstances can change anyone's level of monitoring. A job interview, first meeting, formal event, performance, or sales encounter can encourage careful attention to appearance, wording, and the desired impression.

An urgent practical task can shift attention elsewhere. Someone rushing to buy groceries and prepare a meal may care less about presenting polished clothing or manners at that moment. That situation does not, by itself, establish that the person is always a low self-monitor.

Self-presentation is the attempt to create an impression; self-monitoring concerns sensitivity and behavioural adjustment to the demands of the audience or situation. The concepts are connected without being interchangeable.
