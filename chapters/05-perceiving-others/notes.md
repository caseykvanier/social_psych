# Chapter 5: Perceiving Others

## What this chapter is about

This chapter examines how people build impressions, infer causes of behaviour, and develop habitual explanations for success and failure. It emphasizes that an observer sees only part of the situation and can mistake an interpretation for knowledge of another person.

October 7 reviews slides 9–11 and completes slides 12–29: attribution, biases, explanatory styles, and self-handicapping. Slides 1, 12, 17, and 24 are maps or transitions. ## Slide 1 - Chapter map: perceiving others

### What the slide shows

Four circles identify initial impression formation, inferring dispositions, attribution biases, and individual differences.

### Detailed notes

Person perception concerns how we form impressions and explain other people’s behaviour with limited information. The chapter moves from quickly noticed appearances and nonverbal cues to causal explanations, recurring errors, and a person’s habitual explanatory style. An impression is an interpretation, not direct access to someone’s character or motives.

### Key takeaway

Separate what you observe from what you infer about a person.

## Slide 2 - Assigning causes from limited evidence

### What the slide shows

A photograph shows mismatched brightly coloured shoes and leggings beside a sandy surface. Text says we constantly assign causes to behaviours.

### Detailed notes

The class considers what could explain the mismatched shoes. A viewer may infer a deliberate fashion choice, a child’s preference, or a distinctive personality. Another possibility is a temporary activity in which people exchanged shoes. The visible information does not settle the explanation.

The instructor draws attention to small details such as lacing, clothing, and background. Each can become a cue used to construct a story about who the person is and why they acted. Some observations may be accurate while the resulting causal story remains uncertain. Inferring age, gender, intention, or character from the image is not the same as knowing those things.

The example introduces attribution: explaining an action by assigning it a cause. Later slides distinguish causes attributed to the person from causes attributed to the situation.

### Key takeaway

A vivid first impression can be built from a surprisingly small and ambiguous set of observations.

## Slide 3 - Person perception is reciprocal

### What the slide shows

Arrows run in both directions between “you” and “other person,” showing that both people learn about one another.

### Detailed notes

First impressions develop quickly, and both people contribute to the interaction. Your expression, posture, attention, and response become part of the evidence the other person sees. Their response then changes your experience of them.

The hallway example starts with an ambiguous expression. Someone who has just heard a joke may look cheerful; someone preoccupied by bad news may look unhappy. An observer can turn a momentary expression into a trait judgment. If the observer then responds warmly or coldly, that response may elicit a matching reaction, making the original interpretation seem confirmed.

An apparently unfriendly response can also conceal embarrassment or uncertainty. The point is not that a particular look secretly means attraction. It is that the same observable response has multiple possible causes. You are part of the other person’s social situation, so the impression you receive is not independent of your own behaviour.

### Key takeaway

Impressions emerge within a feedback loop; your behaviour helps shape what you observe.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-06, approximately 00:15:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 4 - Nonverbal behaviour

### What the slide shows

Photographs show facial expression, posture, and interpersonal interaction. A link names a point-light display.

### Detailed notes

Nonverbal cues include movement, gait, facial expression, posture, gaze, and tone of voice. People use them to infer feelings and dispositions, often before much verbal information is available. The lecture describes a point-light display in which moving dots representing body joints can suggest a person’s movement or apparent characteristics. The linked demonstration did not work in class; the instructor substituted a sketch and explanation. These notes describe the concept rather than claiming that the animation was shown.

A trained dancer’s movement is used as an example of how habitual bodily action can create an impression. The lecture also considers an unapproachable expression or headphones as possible ways to discourage unwanted interaction. Such signals are context dependent and do not reveal a single motive.

The instructor explicitly identifies a colloquial gendered label for an unfriendly resting expression as objectionable. The educational point is impression management and the risk of mistaking appearance for mood. A friendly expression never establishes consent or an obligation to interact.

### Key takeaway

Nonverbal cues carry information, but their meaning depends on context and remains open to misinterpretation.

## Slide 5 - Eye-contact norms

### What the slide shows

Eyes filled with national flags accompany the question of how much direct eye contact is appropriate.

### Detailed notes

There is no single amount of eye contact that means the same thing in every setting. During conversation, some gaze can communicate attention, while an unbroken stare can feel intrusive. On public transit, looking away may help maintain privacy. The same action can therefore be interpreted differently depending on relationship, activity, and perceived power.

The instructor discusses cultural and gendered expectations as examples of variation: gaze can be read as respectful attention in one setting and as challenging in another. These are broad classroom illustrations, not rules that determine every member of a culture or gender. Neither gaze nor looking away proves attraction, respect, deception, or hostility.

The lecture also notes that visual or eye-alignment differences can be misread socially. The spoken medical label is not sufficiently precise to treat as a diagnosis; the relevant course point is that an observer can mistakenly interpret a physical difference as an interpersonal signal.

### Key takeaway

Interpret eye contact through the social context, rather than as an automatic sign of a hidden attitude.

## Slide 6 - Nonverbal meaning in text communication

### What the slide shows

A grid of emoji faces illustrates the claim that nonverbal behaviour can improve communication.

### Detailed notes

Words alone may leave tone ambiguous. “Nice sweatshirt” can be sincere or sarcastic depending on delivery, expression, and context. In a text message, emoji and other conventions can try to replace some of those missing cues.

The lecture contrasts communication habits across peer groups and generations. A symbol that seems clear within one group may be interpreted differently by another. An emoji does not remove the need to know the relationship and conversational context. The broader point is that meaning comes from more than literal word content: people communicate stance and emotion through multiple channels.

### Key takeaway

Nonverbal and symbolic cues help interpret words, but shared conventions matter.

## Slide 7 - Greater sensitivity to negative cues

### What the slide shows

An angry face accompanies the statement that people are more sensitive to negative faces and behaviours.

### Detailed notes

Negative feedback or an unfriendly expression can weigh more heavily than several positive signals. The lecture illustrates this with feedback on a piece of writing and evaluations that contain many compliments but one memorable criticism. The example does not establish a fixed numerical ratio between compliments and insults.

A feedback sandwich places a criticism between positive comments to soften its impact. The instructor connects this to the problem of overwhelming a recipient: too much threatening feedback may make the person stop processing it. The course claim concerns the relative salience and interpersonal weight of negative information, not proof that a particular feedback format always improves learning.

Because negative cues can dominate an impression, people often manage their expressions or soften criticism. That can make the information available to an observer selective.

### Key takeaway

A small amount of negative information can have a disproportionate effect on an impression.

## Slide 8 - Detecting deception

### What the slide shows

A simple vertical percentage scale shows a 50% chance baseline and a 54% line for observed performance.

### Detailed notes

The figure illustrates that average deception judgments are only slightly better than chance in the task described. Feeling confident about reading people is not the same as being accurate. The lecture emphasizes that people generally overestimate their ability to detect lies.

The instructor discusses repeated relevant experience and some difficult life circumstances as possible influences on sensitivity to deception. These examples should not be used to diagnose an individual’s history or establish that a profession is infallible. No single gesture or expression is presented as a reliable lie detector.

The later discussion frames lying and detection as competing social pressures: people want their own statements believed while identifying others’ false statements. That is an explanatory illustration, not a demonstration that evolution guarantees accuracy. Read 54% as modest aggregate performance in the illustrated setting, not an individual’s fixed ability.

### Key takeaway

Confidence and a plausible story about someone’s body language do not establish accurate lie detection.

## Slide 9 - Central traits: warm and cold impressions

### What the slide shows

Two circles labelled warm and cold flank the phrase central traits.

### Detailed notes

People combine observations into an overall impression. The lecture uses warmth and coldness as organizing evaluations: several different mixtures of traits can lead to liking, comfort, or distance. Someone may like a grandparent and a friend for different reasons while experiencing both impressions as warm.

Trait information is not simply a list of independent facts. An organizing judgment can influence how other information is understood. Keep the overall evaluation distinct from its evidence: feeling that someone is warm does not establish every positive trait one might associate with that impression. The lecture reviews this connection at the start of October 7 before turning to causal attributions.

### Key takeaway

Different trait combinations can be organized into a broadly warm or cold impression.

## Slide 10 - Primacy and recency in impressions

### What the slide shows

A curve plots weight of information against order of presentation. Weight is higher at the beginning and end and lower in the middle.

### Detailed notes

Primacy is the influence of information encountered early; recency is the influence of information encountered most recently. An initial impression can become the lens for interpreting later behaviour, while a recent encounter remains accessible when judging the current relationship.

The lecture connects the pattern to the familiar memory demonstration in which early and late list items are remembered better, then applies it to longer social relationships. A longstanding favourable impression can coexist with a recent argument that weighs heavily right now. Later experiences can change that balance.

The graph is schematic rather than a prediction that every relationship follows one fixed curve. Primacy concerns order; it is distinct from the halo effect, which concerns a judgment about one trait spreading to other traits.

### Key takeaway

Both early and recent information can receive disproportionate weight in an impression.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-06, approximately 01:00:00-01:12:52; 2026-10-07, approximately 00:00:00-00:15:00._

## Slide 11 - The halo effect

### What the slide shows

A photograph places a bright ring of light above a person’s head, illustrating a halo.

### Detailed notes

A salient evaluation of one trait can colour judgments of other traits. If someone is known to be intelligent, an observer may also assume that they are honest, kind, or attractive without adequate evidence. An attractive appearance can similarly promote positive assumptions about personality. The effect concerns the observer’s inference, not proof that those traits actually go together.

A negative evaluation can spread as well. Learning something discrediting may lead an observer to reinterpret unrelated features or past actions unfavourably. The lecture illustrates this with an initially appealing person whose conversation changes the overall impression, and with a friendship viewed differently after a betrayal. Those examples do not establish that every strong dislike began as friendship.

Distinguish halo from primacy: a first impression can contain a halo effect, but timing and trait spillover are different mechanisms. A clean example shows a judgment on one dimension influencing another unsupported dimension.

### Key takeaway

Do not infer an entire personality from one admired or disliked characteristic.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-06, approximately 01:00:00-01:12:52; 2026-10-07, approximately 00:00:00-00:15:00._

## Slide 12 - Transition to inferring dispositions

The chapter map repeats its four themes.

### Detailed notes

The lecture now asks how an observer explains another person’s behaviour. A disposition is a relatively characteristic feature attributed to the person; a situation includes the circumstances in which the behaviour occurred. The distinction concerns the proposed cause, not whether the action was good or bad.

### Key takeaway

An impression becomes an attribution when it offers a cause for behaviour.

## Slide 13 - Internal and external attributions

### What the slide shows

Two arrows separate the person, labelled internal attribution, from the situation, labelled external attribution.

### Detailed notes

An attribution is an explanation or inference about why an action occurred. An internal or dispositional attribution points to characteristics of the person. An external or situational attribution points to circumstances. Most behaviour reflects both, but observers may give one more weight.

The hallway example illustrates ambiguity. Ignoring someone could be interpreted as a generally unfriendly personality, a deliberate attempt not to interrupt, or distraction by an immediate concern. The classification depends on the cause proposed, not the action alone. Saying “they did not say hello” merely describes behaviour; it does not identify its cause.

Existing liking can influence the explanation. An observer may excuse a friend’s behaviour through circumstances but treat the same action by a disliked person as evidence of character. Conversely, a disliked person’s kindness may be dismissed as an attempt to gain some outside reward. These patterns reveal how prior impressions can shape causal interpretation.

### Key takeaway

Ask whether the explanation locates the cause in a person’s disposition or in the circumstances.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 00:00:00-00:15:00._

## Slide 14 - Unexpected behaviour invites dispositional inferences

### What the slide shows

A person wearing a horse-head mask in a crowd illustrates the statement that unusual behaviour encourages internal attributions.

### Detailed notes

Unexpected behaviour attracts attention and may feel especially informative about who someone is. The lecture asks what observers infer from the horse mask: playful, attention-seeking, unconventional, or irresponsible. Those are interpretations generated from limited evidence, not established traits of the photographed person.

The instructor connects this to building a schema for an unfamiliar person. An unusual action provides a strong anchor because it stands out. A more ordinary action might pass unnoticed. Yet a salient action can still have situational explanations that are not visible. A single image cannot establish the person’s usual character or reasons for wearing the mask.

### Key takeaway

Distinctiveness can increase an action’s influence on an impression without making the resulting inference accurate.

## Slide 15 - Explaining performance

### What the slide shows

A photograph of an answer sheet asks why the viewer performed as they did on an exam.

### Detailed notes

Performance provides a familiar example of competing causal explanations. A person can cite ability, effort, preparation, task difficulty, or luck. The same outcome can therefore be interpreted in several ways, with different consequences for pride, disappointment, and future motivation.

The lecture contrasts a claim about a stable trait with a claim about a temporary preparation choice. “I am generally hardworking” is a dispositional interpretation; “I used this strategy on this occasion” describes a particular action and context. Do not classify every mention of studying, sleep, or motivation solely from one keyword. Identify how the proposed cause is framed.

The educational example concerns causal explanations rather than the actual performance of any particular student. The next slide adds stability as a separate dimension.

### Key takeaway

Different explanations for the same result can imply different expectations about the next attempt.

## Slide 16 - Locus and stability are separate dimensions

### What the slide shows

A two-by-two table crosses internal versus external locus with stable versus unstable causes. Its cells are ability, task difficulty, motivation, and luck.

### Detailed notes

The table treats ability as internal and stable, task difficulty as external and stable, motivation as internal and unstable, and luck as external and unstable. These are the examples as framed by the diagram, not claims that every ability, task, or motivation is permanently fixed.

Locus asks where the explanation places the cause. Stability asks whether the cause is expected to persist over time. A person may feel differently about a setback attributed to a lasting limitation than one attributed to temporary motivation. A success credited to ability may support pride differently from one credited entirely to luck.

The lecture sometimes uses “locus of control” while explaining locus. Location of a cause and ability to control it should not be treated as identical: calling a cause internal does not by itself establish that it is easily changed. The key task is to track locus and persistence independently rather than assume that internal always means stable or external always means temporary.

### Key takeaway

Classify the cause’s location and expected duration separately.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 00:15:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 17 - Transition to attribution biases

### What the slide shows

The chapter map repeats with attribution biases among its sections.

### Detailed notes

The instructor now names recurring patterns already illustrated by the hallway, performance, and race examples. A bias is a tendency in how explanations are selected or weighted. It is not a guarantee that a specific explanation is false; a person or a situation may really matter. The error concerns drawing an unjustified conclusion or systematically overlooking relevant alternatives.

### Key takeaway

Attribution biases concern patterns of explanation, not proof that all personal or situational causes are wrong.

## Slide 18 - Fundamental attribution error

### What the slide shows

A road-rage photograph accompanies the claim that people overestimate personal causes and overlook situations.

### Detailed notes

The fundamental attribution error is the tendency to overemphasize dispositional explanations of other people’s actions while underweighting the situation. Seeing an unsafe lane change and concluding that the driver is generally inconsiderate illustrates the kind of inference. An observer may not know about distraction, visibility, or other immediate circumstances.

Considering alternatives does not mean declaring the behaviour harmless or excusing it. It means separating an observed action from a broad character judgment. The situation may matter even when an action has serious consequences.

A clear example must show the imbalance: the observer attributes another person’s action to character despite relevant situational information. Simply making any internal attribution is not automatically an error. The slide’s core contrast is person versus situation in explanations of others.

### Key takeaway

An observer may mistake a situationally influenced action for evidence of enduring character.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 00:30:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 19 - Actor–observer difference

### What the slide shows

The definition contrasts more personal attributions for others’ behaviour with more situational attributions for one’s own behaviour. A courtroom image accompanies it.

### Detailed notes

The actor–observer difference compares perspectives. The actor knows more about their immediate circumstances and may explain their action through those details. An observer sees the action more directly than its context and may explain it through a trait. In the driving example, “they are inconsiderate” contrasts with “I did not see the vehicle in my blind spot.”

This differs from the fundamental attribution error, which focuses on overweighting person factors when explaining others. Actor–observer requires a comparison between explanations of self and others. It also differs from self-serving bias, which depends on whether the outcome is favourable or unfavourable.

The lecture notes that knowing someone well can make their circumstances more available, reducing the information gap. Treat the pattern as a tendency, not an absolute rule that people never make internal explanations for their own actions.

### Key takeaway

Perspective and available information can change the explanation given for similar behaviour.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 00:30:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 20 - Self-serving bias

### What the slide shows

A celebratory portrait accompanies a definition: internal explanations for success and external explanations for failure.

### Detailed notes

Self-serving bias protects a favourable view of the self by changing causal emphasis with the outcome. “I won because I am fast; I lost because my shoe came untied” illustrates the contrast. It is not simply a preference for external explanations. Success is often credited internally precisely because that supports a positive identity.

The lecture’s business-success example contrasts a story about talent and effort with advantages supplied by resources, family background, education, and useful contacts. Both personal and situational factors may contribute. A favourable self-narrative can selectively emphasize one set and minimize the other.

Distinguish identifying a genuine situational obstacle from showing a bias. Evidence of the pattern comes from selective explanation across successes and failures, or from ignoring relevant evidence in a self-protective direction.

### Key takeaway

Watch whether causal credit and blame change to favour the person explaining their own outcome.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 00:30:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 21 - Group-serving bias

### What the slide shows

A crowd of sports supporters accompanies the same success–failure pattern applied to an ingroup.

### Detailed notes

Group-serving bias extends self-protective explanation to a group with which someone identifies. A fan may attribute a victory to the team’s talent and commitment but attribute a loss to officiating, circumstances, or unfair advantages held by opponents. The group’s outcome contributes to the supporter’s social identity.

The lecture notes that supporters can maintain loyalty even during repeated losses by finding external reasons why the team would otherwise have succeeded. The explanation may feel good without providing a balanced account. A team can genuinely encounter unfairness; the bias is selective weighting in the ingroup’s favour.

This differs from group attribution error. Here the diagnostic feature is favourable explanation of one’s own group’s outcomes, rather than generalizing a few individuals to an entire outgroup.

### Key takeaway

Group identity can influence where people assign credit for wins and blame for losses.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 00:30:00-01:00:00._

## Slide 22 - Group attribution error

### What the slide shows

An observer looks at one person, with arrows extending the judgment to a larger group. The definition warns against generalizing about an outgroup from few individual observations.

### Detailed notes

Group attribution error occurs when limited observations of individuals are extended too readily to the groups they are assumed to represent. A first impression of one visitor, sports fan, or person from a country can become a claim about everyone in the category. That person may not be representative, and the original impression may itself be mistaken.

The lecture discusses the difference between a tiny sample and broader, varied contact. More experience can expose variation that an isolated encounter conceals. A larger sample alone does not guarantee representativeness, so the key caution is the leap from particular people to a broad group.

The instructor connects such overgeneralization to prejudice and discusses contact as relevant to changing impressions. Geographic and political examples in the lecture are illustrations of that argument, not proof of every individual’s attitudes or of a single cause of prejudice. Avoid reproducing an illustrative stereotype as if it were the conclusion.

### Key takeaway

An individual is not a sufficient basis for a confident judgment about a whole group.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 00:45:00-01:00:00._

## Slide 23 - Victim blaming: just-world and defensive attributions

### What the slide shows

Two panels contrast “the world is fair; they probably deserved it” with a defensive wish to deny that the same harm could happen to oneself.

### Detailed notes

Belief in a just world can encourage the inference that people’s outcomes reflect what they deserve. Defensive attributions can protect a sense of personal safety by explaining another person’s misfortune in ways that supposedly separate the observer from the victim. These mechanisms can overlap but highlight different concerns: deserved outcomes versus protection from threat.

The lecture uses sexual assault and homelessness to show how harmful explanations can make an observer feel safer. “That could not happen to me because I would act differently” may reduce fear by assigning responsibility to the victim. This is an explanation of victim blaming, not endorsement of it. Clothing, friendliness, or being in a location does not establish responsibility for another person’s assault.

The homelessness discussion emphasizes that a visible moment does not reveal the person’s history. Housing, resources, social support, and other circumstances can change. Reducing a complex trajectory to a moral character judgment can hide situational causes. A risk-related observation is also not equivalent to proving that a person deserved an outcome.

### Key takeaway

A psychologically comforting explanation can still be an unsupported and harmful attribution of blame.

## Slide 25 - Three dimensions of attributional style

### What the slide shows

Three triangles identify internal versus external, stable versus unstable, and global versus specific attributions.

### Detailed notes

Locus concerns whether the proposed cause lies in the person or the situation. Stability concerns whether it is expected to persist over time. Globality concerns how broadly it applies across domains or situations.

A person can attribute a difficulty to something lasting but limited to one activity, or extend the same explanation to many unrelated areas. Thus stable and global are not synonyms: “I will continue to struggle with this one task” differs from “I will struggle with everything.”

The lecture describes people as having preferred explanatory patterns while retaining the ability to use other explanations. Classification should follow the actual statement. Do not infer all three dimensions from a single negative adjective or from the outcome alone.

### Key takeaway

Location, persistence over time, and breadth across situations are independent questions.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 01:00:00-01:14:37._

## Slide 26 - Negative attributional style

### What the slide shows

The internal, stable, and global ends of the three dimensions are highlighted.

### Detailed notes

For adverse events, a negative attributional style tends to favour internal, stable, and global explanations. A missed invitation can become “I am unlikeable, I will always be unlikeable, and everyone dislikes me,” extending a limited event into a broad judgment about the self.

A task-specific difficulty and an enduring global inability are different claims. The lecture contrasts “This subject is hard for me” with an explanation that treats every difficulty as proof of a general defect. The latter can make a small setback feel much more consequential.

The instructor relates this pattern to low mood and anxious interpretation, but the classroom examples are not diagnostic criteria and cannot identify a mental-health condition. The attributional dimensions describe an explanation, not a clinical assessment.

### Key takeaway

Adverse events can feel more overwhelming when explained as personal, permanent, and pervasive.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 01:00:00-01:14:37._

## Slide 27 - Positive attributional style and its limits

### What the slide shows

The external, unstable, and specific ends of the three dimensions are highlighted.

### Detailed notes

An adverse event can instead be explained as situational, temporary, and limited in scope. A setback after a poor night’s sleep can be interpreted as something that need not determine the next attempt or every other area of life. This may protect motivation and reduce the event’s emotional impact.

The instructor explicitly warns that positive and negative do not simply mean good and bad. Blaming every difficulty on outside forces can prevent realistic responsibility or adjustment. Conversely, acknowledging one’s own contribution can support useful change. A helpful explanation should fit the evidence and distinguish changeable actions from sweeping judgments about worth.

The lecture’s “positive” pattern is illustrated particularly for setbacks. Do not mechanically apply the same combination to every success or assume that external explanations are always more accurate.

### Key takeaway

An explanation can feel better while still needing scrutiny for accuracy and personal agency.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 01:00:00-01:14:37._

## Slide 28 - Unrealistic optimism and learned helplessness

### What the slide shows

Opposing regions place unrealistic optimism with positive attributional style and learned helplessness with negative attributional style, with arrows suggesting movement along a continuum.

### Detailed notes

The lecture uses two extremes to explain why flexibility matters. Unrealistic optimism minimizes real obstacles or assumes success without sufficient preparation. Learned helplessness is illustrated by giving up because effort seems unable to change the outcome. These are broader ideas represented through simplified classroom examples, not clinical diagnoses.

The basketball example contrasts refusing to try something valued because failure feels inevitable with expecting a professional career after almost no experience. The instructor also distinguishes a realistic decision not to pursue an unsuitable or unavailable role from helplessness. Not trying is not by itself evidence of learned helplessness.

A modest degree of optimism is presented as potentially useful, while the main practical point is a balance between hope, evidence, responsibility, and realistic opportunity. The slide does not establish that all optimism is unrealistic or that all negative evaluations indicate helplessness.

### Key takeaway

Look for whether an explanation supports realistic effort or instead promotes avoidance or unjustified certainty.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 01:00:00-01:14:37._

## Slide 29 - Self-handicapping

### What the slide shows

A photograph shows shoelaces from two shoes tied together. The definition concerns creating an external explanation for possible failure.

### Detailed notes

Self-handicapping establishes an excuse or obstacle before a performance so that a later failure can be attributed to something other than ability. It may involve a claimed impediment or behaviour that actually makes success harder. The image of tied shoelaces makes the second possibility concrete.

The lecture mentions announcing tiredness or an old injury before a competition. Such information can sometimes be a self-protective explanation, but a genuine injury or an honest request for accommodation is not automatically self-handicapping. The diagnostic feature is the function of protecting self-evaluation by arranging or emphasizing an alternative explanation ahead of possible failure.

Distinguish timing from self-serving bias after an outcome. Self-handicapping prepares an explanation in advance; an after-the-fact excuse alone does not show that an obstacle was created beforehand.

### Key takeaway

Self-handicapping protects the explanation of failure, potentially at the expense of actual performance.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-07, approximately 01:00:00-01:14:37._
