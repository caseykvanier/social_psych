# Chapter 2: Social Learning and Social Cognition

## What this chapter is about

This chapter connects familiar learning processes to social cognition. Classical conditioning, operant conditioning, and observational learning often operate together in real social situations. The knowledge built through those experiences is organized into schemas. Schemas then shape expectations, attention, interpretation, and behaviour. Because people prefer quick, cognitively easy judgments and prefer to feel correct, prior expectations can become self-reinforcing.

## Slide 2 - Conditioning as a source of social knowledge

### What the slide shows

Learning is introduced through two broad forms of conditioning: operant conditioning, also called instrumental conditioning, and classical conditioning, also called respondent conditioning.

### Detailed notes

**Classical conditioning** is associative learning: a previously neutral cue acquires meaning because it reliably accompanies an important event. It is relatively passive because the learner detects patterns among events in the environment. **Operant conditioning** concerns the consequences of one’s own action. Consequences that improve life make the action more likely to recur; consequences that worsen life make it less likely.

The instructor treats these as interacting processes. A social situation can contain classical associations, consequences that reinforce or punish behaviour, and models whose behaviour is observed at the same time. A phone notification, for example, can become a conditioned cue for a message while checking the phone is reinforced by receiving something rewarding or by ending the anxiety of not knowing what arrived.

### Key takeaway

Do not assume that only one form of learning is operating in a social situation.

## Slide 3 - Classical conditioning of salivation

### What the slide shows

The diagram presents the standard three-stage conditioning sequence. Before conditioning, a tone does not produce salivation. During conditioning, the tone is paired with food, which naturally produces salivation. After repeated pairings, the tone alone produces a conditioned salivation response.

### Detailed notes

Food is an **unconditioned stimulus** because it naturally produces salivation, the **unconditioned response**. Before learning, the tone is a **neutral stimulus** and produces no salivation. Repeatedly pairing tone and food allows the tone to predict food. The tone then becomes a **conditioned stimulus**, and salivation to the tone becomes a **conditioned response**.

The labels depend on the stage of learning. The physical tone is both the neutral stimulus before learning and the conditioned stimulus afterward; salivation is both the unconditioned response to food and the conditioned response to the learned cue.

Socially, a person’s distinctive glasses, a familiar room, a police uniform, or a brand logo can take on the emotional meaning of the person, experience, authority, or product with which it has been paired. A cue can then prepare thoughts, feelings, and behaviour appropriate to that situation.

### Key takeaway

A previously neutral cue can acquire the power to evoke a response after being paired with a meaningful stimulus.

## Slide 4 - Generalization in classical conditioning

### What the slide shows

A graph plots a dog's salivation across piano tones. Salivation is strongest near middle C, the conditioned stimulus, and decreases as tones become less similar to it.

### Detailed notes

Generalization means that learning spreads beyond the exact conditioned cue. Similar cues can produce similar, though usually weaker, responses. A person bitten by one dog may react most strongly to that dog, somewhat to the breed, and more weakly to dogs in general. This transfer is useful: learning that one hot stove can burn should produce caution around similar stoves.

The same process can become socially harmful. An unpleasant interaction with one group member may be generalized to other people categorized as similar, contributing to prejudice even though they had no role in the original event. Generalization is therefore a basic learning tool whose fairness and accuracy depend on whether the similarity is actually relevant.

### Key takeaway

Conditioned responses can transfer to similar stimuli, with response strength usually declining as similarity decreases.

## Slide 5 - Positive and negative reinforcement

### What the slide shows

Positive reinforcement adds a desirable consequence after a response. Negative reinforcement removes or ends an aversive condition after a response. Both make the response more likely to recur.

### Detailed notes

The important distinction is not whether the consequence feels positive or negative. Reinforcement is defined by its effect on future behaviour: the behaviour increases. “Positive” means something is added; “negative” means something is removed.

A compliment or desirable message can positively reinforce an action. Taking pain medication can negatively reinforce pill-taking because the action removes a headache. Fastening a seatbelt is negatively reinforced when it stops an irritating alarm. Socially, apologizing may be negatively reinforced when it ends the tension of friends being angry.

One event can operate on several behaviours at once. Grounding after a failed test may be intended to punish poor academic behaviour, while studying is negatively reinforced because it provides a route out of the unpleasant grounded state. The analysis must specify exactly which behaviour is increasing or decreasing.

### Key takeaway

Positive and negative reinforcement use different consequences, but both increase behaviour.

## Slide 6 - Punishment

### What the slide shows

Punishment involves adding an unpleasant stimulus or removing a pleasant stimulus. The slide gives examples such as criticism, pain, loss of affection, loss of privileges, and license suspension.

### Detailed notes

Punishment is defined by a reduction in the future probability of a behaviour. **Positive punishment** adds an unpleasant consequence, such as criticism. **Negative punishment** removes something desirable, such as a privilege. The terms “positive” and “negative” describe addition and removal, not moral value.

Punishment can be difficult to use effectively because it creates an unpleasant state and may unintentionally reinforce another response that escapes that state. Medication side effects can punish taking an otherwise helpful drug; the delayed rewards of exercise may initially be too weak to offset the immediate soreness that punishes the new routine. Social exclusion or direct criticism may punish behaviour within a friend group, while reconciliation efforts are reinforced when they restore belonging.

### Key takeaway

Reinforcement increases behaviour; punishment decreases it. "Positive" and "negative" refer to adding or removing a consequence, not to whether it is good or bad.

## Slide 7 - Multiple sources of social knowledge

### What the slide shows

The slide lists operant learning, associative learning, and observational learning or modelling.

### Detailed notes

**Observational learning** occurs when people learn by watching what happens to others. Observing someone fall from a dangerous edge can punish the observer’s tendency to approach it without requiring the observer to be injured. Children can acquire fears by watching a parent react fearfully, and athletes can learn complex movement by observing a trusted coach.

Observation can transmit classical associations and operant consequences. People learn which behaviours a group rewards, tolerates, or punishes and then model conduct that appears effective. National rituals, team practices, and family routines can combine repeated cues, shared performance, social approval, and observation.

The lecture emphasizes simultaneous influence. A person may observe a model, form associations with the situation, and experience consequences for their own response. Treating the learning types as interacting processes makes them more useful for explaining complex social behaviour than treating them as separate textbook boxes.

### Key takeaway

Social knowledge can be acquired through consequences, associations, and observation at the same time.

## Slide 8 - Schemas, assimilation, and accommodation

### What the slide shows

The slide introduces schemas and names two ways new information can be incorporated: assimilation and accommodation.

### Detailed notes

A schema is a cognitive structure that holds knowledge and expectations about a category, person, object, or event. Schemas form connected hierarchies: Labrador and husky knowledge sits beneath a broader dog schema, which connects to pet, mammal, and animal schemas. Entering this course similarly creates a social-psychology schema within a larger psychology schema.

Schemas act as filters: they affect which information is noticed, how it is interpreted, and what is later remembered. When meeting someone, people immediately use visible cues, past experience, and broader social categories to fill a sparse person schema. That first impression may contain uncertain guesses but becomes the framework for interpreting what happens next.

**Assimilation** fits new information into an existing schema without changing its basic structure. **Accommodation** changes the schema, or creates a new one, when information does not fit. The September 2 discussion states these definitions correctly at first but briefly reverses the labels during the extended example. These notes follow the definitions printed on slide 9 and flag the verbal inconsistency rather than treating it as a new distinction.

The September 9 lecture further stresses that first impressions and early attitudes are “sticky” because people attend to confirming evidence and discount contradictions.

### Key takeaway

Schemas make the social world easier to interpret, but the same filtering process can make prior beliefs resistant to correction.

## Slide 9 - The nature of remembering

### What the slide shows

The slide defines schemas as frameworks of knowledge and assumptions that affect encoding and recall. It contrasts assimilation, which leaves the core schema intact, with accommodation, which changes or replaces it.

### Detailed notes

Memory is reconstructive rather than a neutral recording. Existing schemas influence what is encoded as important and what can be retrieved later. Because schema-consistent details are easier to connect with what is already known, they may receive more attention and become easier to remember. Inconsistent details may be ignored, explained away, or treated as unimportant.

The first-impression example shows the stakes. A minor behaviour from a person one knows well can be explained as an exception. The same behaviour during an initial meeting may become a defining feature because the observer has little other information. People also deliberately manage early cues, such as interview clothing and manners, because they understand that the opening schema will guide later interpretation.

### Key takeaway

What we remember is partly shaped by what we already believe and expect.

## Slide 10 - Confirmation bias and self-fulfilling prophecy

### What the slide shows

Confirmation bias is the tendency to favour information that supports expectations, whether or not it is true. A self-fulfilling prophecy occurs when expectations change our behaviour toward others in ways that help produce the expected response.

### Detailed notes

The instructor identifies two recurring human tendencies: people like to feel right and prefer not to think harder than necessary. Once a schema produces an expectation, confirming information feels coherent and is easy to accept. Contradictory information is more likely to be ignored, minimized, or rationalized.

The September 2 examples include selectively interpreting a desired person’s behaviour as evidence of reciprocal attraction and evaluating the same grade differently depending on expectations attached to a student category. These examples do not show that the belief is accurate; they show how a belief determines which evidence receives attention and what counts as surprising.

A self-fulfilling prophecy goes one step further because the expectation changes the situation itself. Someone who enters a group expecting hostility may behave defensively or negatively, eliciting colder responses. They then interpret those responses as proof that the group was unfriendly. Someone entering the same group with warmth and openness may elicit friendliness and also conclude that their original expectation was correct. The two people are not merely perceiving the same fixed reality differently; their expectations help create different social interactions.

### Examples and applications

- A negative attitude can elicit unfriendly behaviour and appear to confirm that others are cruel.
- A positive, open attitude can elicit warmer behaviour and appear to confirm that others are friendly.
- Early impressions matter because the first schema provides the starting expectation for later interpretation.

### Key takeaway

Expectations can bias what we notice and can also change our behaviour in ways that make the expectation come true.

## Slide 11 - Automatic cognition, controlled cognition, and priming

### What the slide shows

Automatic cognition is fast, low-effort thinking that occurs largely outside awareness. Controlled cognition is deliberate, effortful evaluation. Priming temporarily increases the accessibility of information and can influence judgment without awareness.

### Detailed notes

Controlled cognition is useful when a problem is important, uncertain, or requires perspective taking. It allows someone to compare explanations, weigh evidence, and plan a response. It is also slow and demanding. Automatic cognition uses learned patterns and immediately accessible information to produce a quick response. It is indispensable when there is no time for careful analysis, but its speed can make it vulnerable to bias.

The restaurant example shows the trade-off. Controlled cognition considers price, health goals, past experience, dietary restrictions, and the restaurant's reputation. Automatic cognition says, "I feel like a burger," and finishes the choice immediately. The football example shows that automatic cognition is not simply careless: a skilled player relies on extensive practice to react to defenders faster than conscious analysis would allow.

The communication medium can shift the balance. Face-to-face conversation often demands an immediate response. Texting introduces a pause that permits more controlled editing and perspective taking. A person may therefore appear different in text than in live conversation.

Priming changes which memories or ideas are easiest to access. News about one natural disaster makes later disasters more noticeable without changing their actual frequency. Seeing a parent before entering a situation may activate family roles and responsibilities; seeing a romantic partner may activate a different social self. Marketing uses repeated exposure, jingles, celebrities, and influencers to make products cognitively accessible at the moment of choice. Once a purchase becomes habitual, the consumer may repeat it with little further thought.

The lecture also applies priming to performance. Making memories of failure accessible before a test may raise anxiety; making memories of success accessible may support a more confident approach. The claim is not that priming determines the result, but that accessible memories alter the cognitive frame brought into the task.

### Key takeaway

Automatic cognition provides speed and efficiency; controlled cognition provides deliberation. Priming influences automatic judgment by changing what comes to mind most easily.

## Slide 12 - Common cognitive shortcuts and biases

### What the slide shows

The table lists cognitive accessibility, salience, representativeness, availability, anchoring and adjustment, counterfactual thinking, false consensus, and overconfidence, with a definition and example for each.

### Detailed notes

These processes are presented as a family of shortcuts rather than as distinctions students must memorize in microscopic detail. The common theme is that judgment is shaped by information that is easy to access, attention-grabbing, or consistent with an existing schema.

- **Cognitive accessibility:** Information near the "surface" of memory receives more weight because it is easier to retrieve.
- **Salience:** Unusual, moving, attractive, motivationally important, or otherwise attention-grabbing stimuli dominate awareness. The lecture examples include a very tall person, an attractively presented person, chocolate cake, and money.
- **Availability heuristic:** Events that are easy to recall are judged as more common or important. Concentrated news coverage of disasters can produce that impression.
- **Representativeness heuristic:** A case is judged by how well it matches a schema or prototype. A golden retriever may feel like a more obvious example of "dog" than a dachshund, even though both are dogs.
- **Anchoring and adjustment:** One accessible starting point receives too much weight, and later correction is insufficient. The instructor notes that students do not need to draw a fine boundary between this and availability for the current assessment.
- **Counterfactual thinking:** People mentally replay an outcome and imagine how a small change might have produced a different result. This can exaggerate personal control and fuel rumination, even when the person made the best decision possible with the information available at the time.
- **False consensus bias:** People use their own accessible beliefs as a starting point and overestimate how widely others share them. The lecture applies this to political and religious opinions and to the logically impossible tendency for most drivers to regard themselves as above average.
- **Overconfidence:** Subjective certainty and objective accuracy can diverge. A memory can feel certain and still be false; someone can believe they performed well socially while others were unimpressed, or the reverse. The lecture flags eyewitness testimony as a later application.

### Assessment guidance from the lecture

Students should be generally familiar with these processes and recognize examples of heuristics. The instructor explicitly says that fine distinctions among every item will not be emphasized.

### Key takeaway

Cognitive shortcuts are efficient, but confidence, accessibility, and attention are not the same as accuracy or importance.

## Lecture-only synthesis

The instructor closes the chapter by returning to two broad principles: people prefer to feel right, and people prefer cognitively easy judgments. These principles connect schemas, confirmation bias, priming, heuristics, and overconfidence. They are more important than memorizing isolated labels without understanding the shared mechanism.
