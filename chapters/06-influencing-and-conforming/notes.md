# Chapter 6: Influencing and Conforming

## What this chapter is about

This chapter asks why people follow groups and authorities, how minorities can change norms, and what gives another person power. It connects classic conformity and obedience examples to the joint roles of the person and the social situation.

## Coverage in the available lectures

October 14 introduces the nature of social power and teaches conformity through slides 1–16, including informational versus normative influence, private acceptance versus public compliance, Asch’s line judgments, majority and minority influence, and situational determinants. October 19 teaches slides 17–37, including obedience, Milgram variations, the lecture’s prison-study account, five power types, and brief person, gender, culture, and reactance discussions. Slides 1–2, 16–17, 27, 33, and 38 are covers, maps, syntheses, or outlines. Slide 25 is an accompanying photograph with no separately identifiable explanation. No separate leadership-style taxonomy is taught. The uncertain Milgram sex-difference aside and unsourced prescription-filling estimate are excluded from assessable content. ## Slide 3 - Social influence and everyday norms

### What the slide shows

Large text defines influence through its effects on everyday thoughts, feelings, and behaviour.

### Detailed notes

Social influence concerns how other people affect the ABCs: affect, behaviour, and cognition. Conformity brings a person’s behaviour or judgment into line with a group’s expectations. It can occur through implicit reactions rather than an explicit order.

The classroom example distinguishes classmates’ disapproving looks at a loud video from an instructor’s formal instruction to stop it. The first illustrates peer pressure around a shared norm; the second adds institutional authority. Both can occur in the same setting.

The lecture also treats gender presentation, manners, and unfamiliar cultural expectations as settings for conformity. A norm violation can receive disapproval or sometimes approval, depending on the group and circumstances. The fact that a behaviour is unconventional does not establish that it will always be punished or celebrated.

### Key takeaway

Social influence can operate through ordinary expectations and reactions, even without a direct command.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:15:00-00:30:00._

## Slide 4 - Is conformity good or bad?

### What the slide shows

A yellow marshmallow figure among green ones is being painted green. The question asks whether conformity is positive or negative.

### Detailed notes

The term conformist may sound negative, but ordinary coordination relies on shared expectations. Being considerate in a classroom or following familiar manners can make social life easier. Blindly following a harmful norm can instead create problems.

The instructor emphasizes that functioning within a group usually involves following many expectations without thinking about them. Entering an unfamiliar setting makes these expectations more visible: social missteps and others’ reactions reveal rules that were previously unknown.

Conformity therefore describes a process, not a moral verdict. Evaluate the norm, the consequences, and the person’s reasons for following it. Rejecting a norm is not automatically beneficial either.

### Key takeaway

Whether conformity is helpful depends on what people are conforming to and what follows.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:15:00-00:30:00._

## Slide 5 - Informational social influence

### What the slide shows

A person appears to ask a driver or official for directions.

### Detailed notes

Informational social influence occurs when people use others as a source of information about what is true or what to do. A lost person seeks directions because another person is believed to know the route. The recipient can actively invite the influence.

The lecture extends this to teaching, coaching, language correction, neighbourhood knowledge, and advice about a decision. Relevant experience, apparent expertise, credentials, trust, and approachability can affect whom someone asks. Those cues explain perceived credibility; they do not guarantee that the answer is correct.

The defining motive is obtaining useful guidance, rather than gaining approval merely by matching the group. The two motives can nevertheless operate together when a newcomer is trying to learn and fit in.

### Key takeaway

Informational influence uses others’ apparent knowledge to reduce uncertainty.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:15:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 6 - Private acceptance

### What the slide shows

A thought bubble and a speech bubble both express liking the Beatles, illustrating agreement between private belief and public statement.

### Detailed notes

Private acceptance means the person actually accepts the belief or judgment, rather than only expressing it outwardly. Thought and public expression align in the cartoon. Informational influence can produce this if the person finds the information convincing.

It is an outcome, not proof of a particular cause. An observer cannot establish private acceptance from a public statement alone. The lecture’s directions example includes both trusting the answer and acting on it, while the music cartoon makes the otherwise hidden private belief visible.

The instructor uses a conversational term transcribed as “concordance” when discussing alignment. The core distinction is clear even though that wording is uncertain: genuine acceptance differs from outward agreement without belief.

### Key takeaway

Private acceptance involves a change or endorsement in what a person actually believes.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:15:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 7 - Normative social influence

### What the slide shows

A group photograph accompanies the normative social influence label.

### Detailed notes

Normative influence concerns fitting in, obtaining approval, or avoiding social rejection. A person may follow what the immediate group is doing even when the activity is not a wider cultural norm. The lecture uses friends suddenly jumping together: the group’s behaviour creates a local pressure not to stand out.

Normative pressure can operate without an explicit threat. Disapproving looks, expected politeness, and the wish to remain part of a valued group can be enough. The person need not believe the group is factually correct.

Distinguish the motive from the visible act. Following others can be informational if they appear to know the answer, normative if approval matters, or both if the person is uncertain and also wants acceptance.

### Key takeaway

Normative influence depends on the social consequences of agreeing or standing apart.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:30:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 8 - Public compliance

### What the slide shows

A thought bubble says the Beatles are overrated while the speech bubble expresses liking them.

### Detailed notes

Public compliance means outward agreement or conformity without corresponding private acceptance. The person in the cartoon says what the group seems to want to hear while retaining a different opinion.

The lecture includes polite compliments and joining a group activity to avoid being the odd one out. These examples show why a public expression cannot by itself establish a private attitude. Compliance can preserve a relationship or avoid discomfort even without changing belief.

Public compliance and normative influence are closely related in these examples but are not synonyms: one names the observed public–private pattern and the other names a motive for influence. A person’s explanation or independent evidence about belief is needed to distinguish the pattern from genuine agreement.

### Key takeaway

Outward agreement does not establish inward belief.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:30:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 9 - A newcomer can need information and acceptance

### What the slide shows

A new group member faces two concerns: not yet knowing the procedures and wanting the group to like them.

### Detailed notes

Joining a class, workplace, party, or community can create both uncertainty and a desire to fit in. Asking an established member how things work seeks informational guidance. Watching others and adjusting to avoid disapproval can reflect normative pressure. The same person can have both motives.

The lecture emphasizes that learning a norm is separate from choosing to follow it. Once a newcomer knows the expectation, they may still reject it. Groups can also develop their own local conventions over time rather than merely inherit one fixed set of rules.

### Key takeaway

Identify both what the newcomer needs to know and what social response they hope to obtain.

## Slide 10 - Majority influence

### What the slide shows

Large groups of figures point inward toward a small group, representing pressure from the majority.

### Detailed notes

Majority influence occurs when a larger group shapes the judgments or actions of an individual or smaller group. The influence can operate through expectations of approval, assumptions that many people must be correct, or both.

The instructor applies the idea to voting within a political party and to shared evaluations of a public figure. Publicly following a party line does not by itself show private endorsement; repeated group agreement may sometimes change the evaluation itself. Party discipline can also involve formal consequences, so that example may contain more than simple peer conformity.

A majority’s numerical size and its unanimity are different features. The following experiment illustrates pressure when the relevant group gives the same answer.

### Key takeaway

A group can influence what a person says, does, or comes to believe.

## Slide 11 - Asch’s line-judgment task

### What the slide shows

A standard card contains one vertical line. A test card contains three lines labelled 1, 2, and 3; line 1 visibly matches the standard.

### Detailed notes

The lecture describes a participant answering a simple perceptual question in a group whose other members have been instructed to give the same wrong response. The participant can see the match but faces an apparently unanimous contrary judgment. The design separates what seems perceptually clear from the social pressure created by the group.

Some people may publicly give the group’s answer to avoid standing out. Others may become uncertain about their perception and accept that the group could be right. The same incorrect public response can therefore reflect different private processes.

The line cards illustrate the task rather than prove that every participant will conform. The study’s social setup and the meaning of the response matter; the result is not a claim that human vision cannot compare lines.

### Key takeaway

A simple task can still create conflict between one’s judgment and an apparently unanimous group.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:45:00-01:00:00._

## Slide 12 - Two different conformity percentages

### What the slide shows

Large numbers report 76% of participants conforming and 37% of responses conforming.

### Detailed notes

The two percentages use different denominators. The lecture explains that 76% of participants conformed at least once, while 37% of the responses in the illustrated conformity judgments followed the group. Someone can conform on one occasion and resist on others, so these figures are compatible.

Do not describe 76% as conforming on every trial or 37% as the proportion of people who ever conformed. The statistics also do not show what each individual privately believed. Distinguishing public compliance from private acceptance requires additional evidence beyond the answer itself.

The course point is both the strength of the group’s influence and its limits: conformity occurred, but it was not uniform across people or occasions.

### Key takeaway

Keep people, responses, and private beliefs distinct when interpreting conformity evidence.

## Slide 13 - Minority influence

### What the slide shows

Arrows point outward from a small group toward the larger surrounding group.

### Detailed notes

A minority can sometimes change a majority’s position. Initial disagreement can create social costs for the dissenter, but a sustained argument may attract support and eventually alter the group’s norm. The lecture presents this as less common than majority influence, not impossible.

A person challenging accepted teasing within a friendship group provides a small-scale example. Civil-rights and queer-rights movements illustrate longer-term challenges to prevailing social norms. The examples show that a current majority does not determine what future group opinion must be.

Minority status alone does not make a view correct or persuasive. The next slide considers features that may make a minority harder to dismiss.

### Key takeaway

Influence can run from a small group toward a larger one, sometimes changing the norm itself.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:45:00-01:13:14._

## Slide 14 - What can make a minority influential?

### What the slide shows

The slide lists consistent and unanimous responses, investment in the position, and appearing to act on principle.

### Detailed notes

A minority can gain influence when its position is consistent over time and its members communicate a coherent stance. Visible commitment and willingness to bear costs can signal that the position is serious. Appearing to act from principle may make the argument harder to dismiss as a passing personal preference.

The lecture contrasts an arbitrary preference with an argument that a practice is unjust. It also notes that movements may share a broad principle while disagreeing about details. Consistency is not the same as literally identical opinions on every issue.

These features do not guarantee success or establish that the position is correct. A person can be committed to a poor argument. The course question is which features might affect the majority’s response, while keeping evidence quality separate from social influence.

### Key takeaway

Consistency, coherence, commitment, and perceived principle can support minority influence without guaranteeing it.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:45:00-01:13:14._

## Slide 15 - Situational determinants of conformity

### What the slide shows

Three panels identify majority size, unanimity, and task importance. The task panel distinguishes easy low-importance tasks from difficult high-importance tasks.

### Detailed notes

A larger majority can make its judgment seem more valid and make dissent more difficult. A unanimous majority can leave a person isolated; an ally can reduce that isolation even when the group is still numerically larger. Size and unanimity therefore need separate attention.

Task difficulty and importance interact with motives. On an easy, low-stakes task, someone may go along because resisting feels unnecessary. On a difficult and important task, an uncertain person may rely on others for information. The slide does not imply that every important decision produces more conformity: confidence, evidence, and the person’s own view also matter.

The lecture applies these ideas to online communities, where a broad public may fragment into smaller but highly unanimous groups, and to financial consensus, where following or challenging a popular view can itself involve social influence. These are applications of the concepts rather than a verified description of every online group or a financial recommendation.

### Key takeaway

Separate majority size, unanimity, uncertainty, and stakes before predicting conformity.

## Slide 18 - Social power

### What the slide shows

A police officer and a person beside a vehicle illustrate the capacity to create conformity even when the person might resist.

### Detailed notes

Social power is the ability to influence another person despite possible resistance. It is relational: influence can depend on access to resources, capacity to impose costs, accepted authority, admiration, or expertise. A consequence need not be explicitly threatened every time for the relationship to matter.

The October 14 introduction considers government, family, peer groups, and classrooms. Dependence on support can give another person leverage; greater independence can change that leverage. A high-status peer may influence reputation without holding legal authority. A teacher’s institutional role and control over valued outcomes can affect interactions even when no sanction is threatened. These are general examples, not a disclosure of any student’s circumstances.

October 19 uses a police encounter to illustrate several sources of power at once. The example is about perceived authority and consequences, not a statement of the legal conditions under which force is permitted. Later slides distinguish the five power types.

### Key takeaway

Power can shape an interaction through understood leverage as well as explicit commands.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-14, approximately 00:00:00-00:30:00; 2026-10-19, approximately 00:00:00-00:15:00._

## Slide 19 - Why study obedience?

### What the slide shows

A historical quotation about crimes committed in obedience appears above a photograph of Nazi personnel. The image introduces the moral problem of following unjust authority.

### Detailed notes

The lecture asks how people can participate in harmful actions while viewing themselves as following a legitimate role or duty. The point is to investigate the social conditions of obedience rather than assume that anyone who commits harm belongs to a wholly different kind of person.

This framing motivates Milgram’s experiment. Understanding situational pressure does not remove responsibility, establish that every individual would behave the same way, or equate a laboratory task with historical atrocities. It challenges confidence that merely imagining oneself in a situation is enough to predict how one would act there.

### Key takeaway

Explaining harmful obedience requires examining the authority relationship and the situation, alongside the person.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-19, approximately 00:00:00-00:15:00._

## Slide 20 - Milgram: roles and experimental setup

### What the slide shows

A recruitment advertisement accompanies a diagram with experimenter (E), teacher (T), and learner (L). The teacher faces a shock apparatus while the learner is shown in another room.

### Detailed notes

The participant takes the teacher role in what is presented as a learning-and-memory study. The learner is a confederate: someone working with the researchers. The experimenter instructs the teacher to increase an apparent electric shock after wrong answers. The learner’s distress is simulated, and the learner is not receiving the escalating shocks.

The crucial distinction is between what happened and what the participant believed. The participant is meant to believe the shocks and distress are real while an authority presses for continuation. Behaviour in that perceived situation is the focus of the obedience question.

Identify all three roles accurately. The experimenter issues the instructions; the participant is the teacher who operates the apparatus; the learner appears to receive its consequences. The study is not simply an observation of people voluntarily choosing to harm strangers with full knowledge that the task is fictitious.

### Key takeaway

The obedience test depends on the teacher’s belief in the situation and the experimenter’s direction.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-19, approximately 00:00:00-00:15:00._

## Slide 21 - The shock apparatus and conflict about continuing

### What the slide shows

A photograph shows the simulated shock generator, controls, cables, and a small recording or communication device.

### Detailed notes

The apparatus made the teacher role concrete: wrong answers were followed by actions on a machine labelled with increasing shock intensity. In the lecture’s described video, a participant expresses concern yet continues when the experimenter directs him. Expressing distress and refusing are different behavioural outcomes.

The lecture reports that about two-thirds continued to the highest level in the initial setup. Some participants stopped. Thus the demonstration concerns substantial obedience under particular conditions, not universal compliance. The apparatus and instructions must be considered with the perceived institutional setting and the participant’s understanding of the task.

The simulated nature of the shocks does not mean that the participant knowingly pretended to comply. The question concerns what the participant believed they were doing.

### Key takeaway

Concern about harm can coexist with continued obedience; some participants nevertheless refuse.

## Slide 22 - Milgram variations: the situation changes obedience

### What the slide shows

A table compares obedience percentages across variants: the initial Yale setting is shown near 65%, an off-campus setting near 48%, sharing a room with the learner near 40%, several more immediate or less authoritative settings near 20%, peers refusing near 10%, and conditions allowing free choice or instructing no shock near zero.

### Detailed notes

The table shows that obedience changes with the setup. Moving away from a prestigious university setting reduces the institutional cue. Bringing the learner closer, especially requiring contact with the learner’s hand, makes the apparent consequences harder to distance oneself from. Moving the experimenter away or replacing the experimenter with an ordinary person changes the immediacy or perceived standing of the authority.

Other participants refusing can model an available alternative. Free choice of shock level and an experimenter’s instruction not to shock sharply reduce the outcome represented by the table. The listed percentages are the slide’s rounded values; they are not one universal obedience rate or an account of every possible variation.

Keep the two proximity effects separate: proximity to the authority can sustain obedience, whereas proximity to the person apparently harmed can reduce it. The lecture emphasizes the direction of these comparisons more than memorizing a full numerical table.

### Key takeaway

Changing authority, distance, choice, or modeled dissent can substantially change obedience.

## Slide 23 - Obedience depends on person and situation

### What the slide shows

The relationship “behaviour = f(person, social situation)” appears above two lists. Person factors include authoritarianism, conscientiousness, agreeableness, moral reasoning, and social intelligence. Situational factors include proximity, choice, and modeled dissent.

### Detailed notes

The lecture combines situational influences with possible individual differences. Authoritarianism is discussed as comfort with rigid hierarchies and following orders. Agreeableness may support compliance when pleasing the experimenter matters. Conscientiousness and moral reasoning are presented as possible supports for resisting an action judged wrong, while social intelligence may help someone recognize manipulation. These are lecture-described tendencies, not deterministic rules or evidence that one trait reveals a person’s moral worth.

The situation supplies the particular role, cues, and consequences in which those tendencies operate. Having others refuse can make dissent easier; having the authority physically present can make refusal socially harder. The lecturer also uses coercive initiation rituals as a possible analogy for pressure on someone seeking membership in a group. That is not a claim about all teams or groups.

The instructor is explicitly uncertain about the direction of a sex difference in the Milgram research and tells students not to rely on the suggestion that women went further. Do not treat that comment, or the slide’s bare listing of sex, as an established taught directional finding.

### Key takeaway

Person factors can influence response, while the arrangement of the situation changes the pressures faced.

## Slide 26 - Prison roles, the environment, and individual differences

### What the slide shows

The person–situation relationship reappears. Person factors include authoritarianism, social dominance orientation, aggression, altruism, and empathy; situational factors list guard, prisoner, warden, superintendent, and an authoritarian environment.

### Detailed notes

In the lecture’s account of the Stanford Prison Experiment, students were randomly assigned to guard or prisoner roles in a simulated prison. Uniforms, arrest procedures, confinement, and control over daily activities helped define unequal positions. The instructor describes initial awkwardness, prisoner resistance, escalating guard harassment, and the researcher becoming absorbed in a warden role. An outside observer recognized the harm and helped bring the study to an early stop.

The lecturer treats the abuse as ethically unacceptable and emphasizes that the researcher was part of the situation rather than an entirely detached observer. The account is an illustration of roles and environment; it should not be treated as a clean demonstration that random role assignment alone inevitably makes every guard cruel. The available lecture does not review the wider methodological debate about this study.

Authoritarianism, dominance orientation, and aggression are discussed as possible contributors to cruelty; empathy and altruism as tendencies in the other direction. These operate within roles and circumstances. The instructor extends the analysis to milder hierarchies in teams, cliques, and organizations without equating those settings with imprisonment.

### Key takeaway

Roles, institutional arrangements, person factors, and the researcher’s own position all matter in interpreting the study.

## Slide 27 - Five types of power

### What the slide shows

A central power circle connects to reward, coercive, legitimate, referent, and expert power.

### Detailed notes

The five labels identify bases of influence. Reward power controls desirable outcomes; coercive power controls unwanted consequences; legitimate power depends on accepting a right to direct; referent power depends on identification or admiration; expert power depends on perceived relevant knowledge.

One person or institution can have several bases at once. The task is to identify which reason for compliance is operating in a particular example. A job title, uniform, or profession alone does not establish a unique answer. The following slides supply the contrasts.

### Key takeaway

Classify the basis for influence, and allow more than one type when the evidence supports it.

## Slide 28 - Reward power

### What the slide shows

A sign reserves a parking space for the employee of the month.

### Detailed notes

Reward power comes from control over something another person values. Recognition, salary, prizes, or a preferred parking space can motivate action. The recipient may willingly pursue the benefit, but the ability to allocate it is still a source of influence.

The lecture includes workplace and educational examples. The important mechanism is the contingent desirable outcome, not whether the powerful person seems kind. A manager may possess reward power and other forms of authority at the same time. The employee-of-the-month sign illustrates a reward, not evidence that recognition always improves performance.

### Key takeaway

Control over a valued benefit can give someone influence over behaviour.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-19, approximately 00:30:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 29 - Coercive power

### What the slide shows

A photograph accompanies the coercive power label, emphasizing restriction or punishment.

### Detailed notes

Coercive power influences through a threatened or applied unwanted consequence. The lecture gives examples such as fines, detention, suspension, grounding, or losing access to a phone. Compliance can occur because the person wants to avoid the cost, even when they reject the demand itself.

Coercion need not involve physical violence. A meaningful restriction or financial penalty can be enough. Distinguish a possible punishment from an accepted right to rule: a person may obey solely because of the penalty while thinking the authority’s decision is wrong. Conversely, an authority may have coercive power that it does not use in a particular interaction.

### Key takeaway

Avoiding punishment is a different reason to comply from accepting a demand as rightful.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-19, approximately 00:30:00-01:00:00._

## Slide 30 - Legitimate power

### What the slide shows

An institutional interior accompanies the legitimate power label.

### Detailed notes

Legitimate power depends on accepting a person or institution as having a rightful authority within a domain. The lecture contrasts accepting a court’s decision as authoritative with following it only to avoid a penalty. The former illustrates legitimacy; the latter may primarily reflect coercion.

Accepting a procedure’s authority does not require agreeing with every outcome. A person may regard a court, teacher, parent, or team captain as entitled to make certain decisions while disliking a particular choice. The scope of that accepted role matters.

The same institution can have several bases of power. A court can issue sanctions and also be regarded as legitimate. Do not force an exclusive classification based solely on the institution’s name; identify the reason the person accepts or follows the particular direction.

### Key takeaway

Legitimacy concerns an accepted right to direct, rather than compliance through consequences alone.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-19, approximately 00:45:00-01:00:00._

## Slide 31 - Referent power

### What the slide shows

A supporter wears a Colin Kaepernick jersey, illustrating identification with a public figure.

### Detailed notes

Referent power arises when people respect, identify with, admire, or want to emulate someone. The person need not control a formal reward or penalty. Their symbolic position can affect what followers consider worthwhile or acceptable.

The lecture discusses Kaepernick’s protest as an example of a public stand that others may identify with or imitate, then extends the concept to friends and online influencers. The influence concerns the audience’s relationship to the person. It does not prove that the person’s advice is expert or that every follower agrees on every issue.

An influencer may have both referent power and perceived expertise. To distinguish them, ask whether the recipient follows because they identify with the communicator or because they believe the communicator knows the subject.

### Key takeaway

Admiration and identification can create influence without formal authority.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-19, approximately 00:45:00-01:00:00._

## Slide 32 - Expert power

### What the slide shows

A medical professional examining a child illustrates influence through relevant knowledge.

### Detailed notes

Expert power depends on accepting another person as having useful knowledge or skill in the relevant area. A patient may follow a prescription or physiotherapy recommendation because they trust the professional’s expertise. The same person can reject advice if they do not accept that expertise.

The lecture emphasizes that medical advice does not automatically compel adherence. Not acting on a recommendation also does not prove disbelief: cost or practical barriers may prevent action. The instructor gives a rough prescription-filling estimate without a source or methodological detail; these notes do not treat that number as a reliable course statistic to memorize.

Expert and legitimate power can overlap. Expertise concerns the knowledge believed relevant to the decision; legitimacy concerns the accepted right to direct. Neither is inferred simply from observing compliance without understanding its reason.

### Key takeaway

Expert power operates through perceived relevant knowledge, and following advice still depends on circumstances.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-19, approximately 00:45:00-01:00:00._

## Slide 33 - Transition to differences in conformity

### What the slide shows

The chapter map repeats, now leading to differences in conformity.

### Detailed notes

The final section asks how tendencies and social expectations affect conformity. It connects the person factors discussed in the studies with the consequences a social setting attaches to dissent.
