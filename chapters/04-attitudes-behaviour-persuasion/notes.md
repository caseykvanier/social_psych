# Chapter 4: Attitudes, Behaviour, and Persuasion

## What this chapter is about

This chapter examines what attitudes are, when they predict behaviour, how messages persuade, and how our own behaviour can reshape our evaluations. It connects the affective, behavioural, and cognitive components of attitudes with social norms, perceived control, and the desire for consistency.

## Coverage in the available lectures

The September 28 lecture teaches attitudes and their behavioural prediction (slides 1–10), then introduces the persuasion routes (slides 11–13). September 30 reviews those routes and covers message processing, communicators, resistance to persuasion, self-perception, justification, and cognitive dissonance through the payment experiment (slides 12–28). October 6 reviews dissonance, completes postdecisional dissonance and persuasion techniques (slides 29–32), then begins Chapter 5. Slides 1, 2, 11, 17, 21, and 22 are maps or outlines. All slide numbers use PDF page order. Terminology cautions on slides 8, 16, 24, 31, and 32 distinguish the lecture’s examples from stronger or ambiguous claims.

## Slide 1 - Chapter map: attitudes, persuasion, and behaviour

### What the slide shows

Three connected circles identify exploring attitudes, changing attitudes through persuasion, and changing attitudes through behaviour.

### Detailed notes

The chapter asks how evaluations of the world connect to action and how those evaluations change. The September 28 introduction revisits the ABC framework: affect concerns feelings, behaviour concerns actions, and cognition concerns thoughts and beliefs. Attitudes interact with all three. Persuasion can change what people believe or feel and thereby affect what they do; acting differently can also change what people believe about themselves.

The lecture initially sometimes uses “attitudes, behaviour, and cognition” as conversational shorthand. The formal A in the ABC framework is affect. Keep that distinction when using the terms.

### Key takeaway

Influence can run from thoughts and feelings to action, and from action back to attitudes.

## Slide 2 - Exploring attitudes

### What the slide shows

An outline lists attitude, the ABCs, attitude strength, and attitude consistency.

### Detailed notes

An attitude is an evaluation directed toward an attitude object. This section distinguishes having an opinion from holding a strong, readily accessible evaluation. The main questions are how strongly the evaluation is held, whether its components fit together, and when it predicts behaviour. Subsequent slides develop each idea; this outline is not a separate mechanism.

### Key takeaway

Attitudes differ in strength, accessibility, and consistency.

## Slide 3 - Attitude and attitude object

### What the slide shows

The definition describes an attitude as a relatively enduring evaluation of something. A photograph with an inspirational statement illustrates a generally positive outlook.

### Detailed notes

The attitude object is whatever is being evaluated: a food, a political position, a person, an activity, or an institution. An evaluation may be positive, negative, or relatively neutral. “Relatively enduring” does not mean permanent. Experiences and social contexts can change both an attitude and which attitude is most prominent.

The lecture contrasts a mild food preference with political or religious commitments that help define a person. Trying blue cheese in a different context illustrates change: new behaviour and a new interpretation of the experience can shift an initially negative evaluation. These are examples of attitude objects, not proof that every preference is equally central to identity.

### Key takeaway

Identify both the evaluation and the object toward which it is directed.

## Slide 4 - Attitude strength

### What the slide shows

A definition connects attitude strength with importance and speed of coming to mind. A repeated “I am vegan” image illustrates a potentially identity-relevant attitude.

### Detailed notes

Some attitudes are weak and easily changed; others are personally important and strongly defended. Speed of coming to mind is an indicator of accessibility, one contributor to strength. Do not treat importance and accessibility as identical in every situation.

Veganism is the lecture’s extended example. Beliefs about animal welfare and nutrition provide a cognitive component, emotions give it personal significance, and shopping, cooking, checking menus, and bringing food create behavioural commitments. Repeatedly acting in line with an evaluation can reinforce it. The point is a possible relationship between commitment and strength, not a claim that all vegans hold identical attitudes or motivations.

A casual opinion about a singer may change after hearing a new song. A devoted fan has more identity invested and may defend the same singer against criticism. The size of a disagreement’s personal impact depends partly on how much the attitude matters.

### Key takeaway

Strong attitudes tend to be important, accessible, and supported by experience or commitment.

## Slide 5 - What strengthens an attitude?

### What the slide shows

Three bullets name accessibility, direct experience, and aligned ABCs.

### Detailed notes

Accessibility concerns how readily an evaluation comes to mind now. Someone can value ethical purchasing and also enjoy a convenient food. Hunger may make the food-related evaluation more accessible; a social event centred on a moral commitment may make the ethical evaluation more accessible. This helps explain inconsistency across situations without assuming the person has only one attitude.

Direct experience connects an attitude to consequences in daily life. The lecture contrasts abstract debates with a resident’s concern about repeated local flooding: a personally experienced problem may produce a more immediate, strongly felt evaluation. This example does not establish a general ranking of political issues.

Aligned ABCs mean that feelings, beliefs, and actions support one another. Repeated action consistent with an evaluation can strengthen it. A mismatch can create cognitive dissonance and pressure to change either the behaviour or the attitude. The relevant attitude may therefore change in strength as the social context changes.

### Key takeaway

Accessibility, lived experience, and alignment help explain why some evaluations guide action more reliably.

## Slide 6 - Attitude consistency and the ABCs

### What the slide shows

Arrows connect behaviour, feelings, and thoughts around core beliefs and references to self, others, and future. The caption says consistency increases attitude strength.

### Detailed notes

Attitude consistency means that affective, behavioural, and cognitive components point in the same direction. A student may value education, feel pride or belonging, and attend class and study. When these fit together, the student role is easier to sustain. If the person comes to believe the activity is pointless or feels alienated, continuing the same behaviour may become uncomfortable. This is an illustrative mechanism, not a judgment about any individual’s attendance.

The relationship example makes the same point. Enjoying time with someone, thinking well of them, and choosing to see them reinforce one another. Conflict can disrupt that pattern. A new balance could involve changed interpretations, improved feelings, or spending less time together. Consistency is a tendency toward alignment, not a promise that every relationship or attitude has a simple resolution.

### Key takeaway

Consistency concerns agreement among what a person feels, thinks, and does.

## Slide 7 - When self-reports predict behaviour

### What the slide shows

A list identifies strong attitudes, strong intentions, specific attitudes, and low self-monitoring as conditions supporting prediction.

### Detailed notes

Asking someone about an attitude is straightforward, but predicting later action requires more than obtaining an answer. Strong intentions are less easily displaced by competing opportunities. Planning vaguely to study sometime is less informative than having a firm intention tied to an immediate need. Even a sincere intention can fail to become action.

Specificity also matters. A broad evaluation of a category is less closely matched to a particular future action than an evaluation naming that action and its timeframe; slide 10 develops the evidence.

Low self-monitors adjust their presentation less to what they think an audience wants. Their expressed attitudes may therefore align more closely with their own evaluations and actions. High self-monitoring does not simply mean dishonesty: a social setting can make a role or norm salient and change what feels appropriate. Interviews are more visibly social than anonymous questionnaires, but socially desirable answers can occur in either format.

### Key takeaway

Match the attitude measure to the action, and consider strength, intention, and social presentation.

## Slide 8 - Assessing attitudes

### What the slide shows

The slide lists self-report, implicit measures, and neuroimaging.

### Detailed notes

Self-report directly asks what someone thinks or feels. It is useful but vulnerable to social desirability: a respondent may give an acceptable answer rather than express a stigmatized evaluation. The lecture contrasts an anonymous questionnaire with a face-to-face interview and explains that a visible audience can activate a social role.

Indirect approaches examine responses other than a direct declaration. The lecture mentions physiological stress, interpretations of ambiguous scenes, and beliefs attributed to other people as possible clues. These are illustrations of indirect inference, not reliable individual diagnoses. Stress is not equivalent to lying, and the instructor explicitly cautions that lie detection does not work as a simple truth machine.

Neuroimaging can investigate patterns of response under controlled conditions; it cannot literally read a person’s mind. The lecture briefly refers to the Implicit Association Test but does not demonstrate its procedure. It then describes ambiguous-scene or projection tasks. Those are different procedures and should not be conflated with the Implicit Association Test. This terminology boundary matters more than memorizing an unsupported test description.

### Key takeaway

Every attitude measure requires interpretation; neither self-report nor an indirect measure provides infallible access to private beliefs.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-28, approximately 00:30:00-01:00:00._

## Slide 9 - Theory of planned behaviour

### What the slide shows

Attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioural control feed into intention, which points to behaviour. A dashed path links perceived control with behaviour.

### Detailed notes

The theory connects evaluations to action through intention and perceived ability to act. Attitude concerns the evaluation of the proposed action. Subjective norm concerns perceived expectations or approval from relevant others. Perceived behavioural control concerns whether the person thinks they have the resources and opportunity to do it. These factors help explain intention; intention does not guarantee behaviour.

The lunch example separates liking a restaurant from whether friends approve, whether time allows a visit, and whether the person can pay. The expensive-shoes example adds a social role: the same shoes may confer status in one group and seem inappropriate or unimportant in another. Having money supports control; expecting group approval supports subjective norm; valuing design or exclusivity supports attitude. A sold-out website can still prevent purchase despite a strong intention.

Marketing can address several components. A sale makes a purchase feel more affordable, favourable product claims address evaluation, and advertisements showing valued peers can shift perceived norms. These factors interact, but do not collapse them into one generic desire to buy.

### Key takeaway

Separate evaluation, perceived social expectations, perceived control, intention, and actual action.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-28, approximately 00:45:00-01:12:47._

## Slide 10 - Specific attitudes predict specific behaviour better

### What the slide shows

A bar graph plots correlation on the vertical axis and increasingly specific attitude questions on the horizontal axis. Bars rise from roughly .08 for birth control generally to about .55 for personally using pills within two years.

### Detailed notes

The four questions move from attitudes toward birth control, to birth-control pills, to personally using pills, to personally using them during the next two years. The relationship with the corresponding behaviour becomes stronger as the question matches the action more closely. The graph’s largest correlation remains well below a perfect relationship.

The instructor describes the upper value, about .55, as moderate in this discussion. The important course point is incomplete prediction, not an invariant verbal cutoff for correlation strength. Intending to return a library book or renew a transit pass does not ensure that the action happens on time. A more specific attitude measure improves prediction without eliminating practical barriers, forgetting, or changes in circumstances.

This is a graph about association and measurement specificity. It does not show that asking a more specific question by itself causes the later behaviour.

### Key takeaway

Greater specificity improves the match between an attitude measure and behaviour, but prediction remains imperfect.

## Slide 12 - Spontaneous and thoughtful processing

### What the slide shows

Spontaneous processing is paired with peripheral or heuristic processing; thoughtful processing is paired with central or systematic processing.

### Detailed notes

Two routes can influence attitudes. Central, systematic processing involves considering the content and merits of an argument. Peripheral, heuristic processing relies more on cues such as appeal, status, emotional association, or a simple shortcut. Both can produce persuasion; they do not describe two permanent kinds of people.

The September 28 discussion contrasts carefully researching a major purchase with responding to a product’s associations. A person who reasons carefully about one decision can rely on cues in another. The next lecture returns to motivation and ability, which affect whether a person elaborates the message.

The September 30 lecture contrasts a lively rally with a sustained discussion of policy. Both may shift an immediate intention, but the instructor emphasizes that a thoughtfully elaborated attitude tends to be more durable. A short-term sales goal can instead exploit an immediate feeling. These are tendencies, not a guarantee that one particular person will act or later regret a choice.

### Key takeaway

Distinguish attention to argument quality from reliance on persuasive cues.

## Slide 13 - Elaboration likelihood: motivation and ability

### What the slide shows

A car advertisement sits between two paths. The central path lists need, price, performance, and reviews; the peripheral path lists wanting a car and attraction to the surrounding image. Motivation and ability appear above the contrast.

### Detailed notes

The lecture’s car example separates a product’s relevant merits from its image. Under the central route, a buyer evaluates need, price, performance, and comparative evidence. Under the peripheral route, the buyer may respond to status, attractive imagery, or the kind of life associated with ownership. A luxury purchase can involve both.

Researching specifications does not mean a person is immune to social meanings. Conversely, buying a relatively inexpensive product does not prove that the person used careful reasoning. The mechanism concerns which information drives the evaluation. A buyer may justify a status-oriented choice with technical details, so the explanation requires more than observing the price.

The instructor emphasizes that an expensive luxury car need not offer proportionally greater practical performance. Its symbolic role can contribute to its appeal. This is an illustrative comparison from the lecture rather than a technical evaluation of particular car brands.

### Key takeaway

Ask what actually persuaded the person: evaluated evidence, peripheral cues, or a combination.

## Slide 14 - Two paths from message to attitude change

### What the slide shows

One flow diagram runs from message through an emotional response to attitude change. A second inserts cognitive elaboration between message and change.

### Detailed notes

The spontaneous path works through the immediate affective response or a persuasive cue. The thoughtful path involves cognitive elaboration: considering information, comparing options, relating an argument to one’s needs, and being able to explain a choice.

The lecture applies the distinction to product choices, program choices, and requests of a parent. A minor immediate request may succeed through affection or convenience; an expensive trip may require a defensible argument about costs and benefits. The same relationship can involve both routes. A request’s importance, the recipient’s attention, and the surrounding situation affect which route matters most.

The diagram is a simplified contrast. It does not establish that emotion is absent from careful reasoning, or that every quick decision lacks a reason.

### Key takeaway

Cognitive elaboration concerns working through the message rather than relying primarily on how it feels or who presents it.

## Slide 15 - Effective communicators

### What the slide shows

A list includes attractiveness, similarity, trustworthiness, likeability, expertise, fast speech, and speaking against self-interest.

### Detailed notes

A message’s source can become part of its persuasive force. Attractive or admired communicators may motivate a listener to gain their approval. Similarity can make advice feel relevant: a peer may seem to understand the listener’s needs better than someone in a very different role. Likeability, apparent expertise, and trust can support willingness to listen. They do not prove that the argument is correct.

The lecture’s clothing-store example contrasts indiscriminate flattery with a salesperson who compares options and admits that one looks less suitable. An apparent concession can make the recommendation seem more credible. Speaking against self-interest means appearing to prioritize the customer’s needs over a sale or commission; it can itself be a sales tactic.

Quick speech may signal confidence or leave less time to scrutinize an argument. Tone and delivery also matter. These are possible influences, not a rule that faster is always better or a reason to equate fluency with expertise.

### Key takeaway

Evaluate the message’s evidence separately from the communicator’s appeal and apparent credibility.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-30, approximately 00:00:00-00:30:00._

## Slide 16 - The sleeper effect

### What the slide shows

A graph plots attitude over time. A line labelled expert stays high and flat; a line labelled non-expert rises toward it. The caption describes remembering a message while forgetting its initially distrusted source.

### Detailed notes

The sleeper effect is a delayed increase in a message’s influence after it was initially discounted because of its source. A person may remember the claim but lose the connection to the reason for distrusting it. The instructor uses repeated online rumours and conspiratorial claims as illustrations of this possibility. The educational point is source–message separation, not endorsement of the claims.

The lecture also distinguishes mere persistence from this mechanism. Repeatedly asking for a purchase may wear someone down, but repetition alone does not establish a sleeper effect. The relevant evidence is initial discounting followed by later influence as the source or discounting cue becomes less accessible.

There is a terminology ambiguity on this slide: the graph labels can be read as communicator expertise, while the spoken explanation also contrasts knowledgeable recipients with non-experts. Preserve the central mechanism without using those labels to claim that experts are universally immune. The generic slide definition “attitude change over time” is broader than the sleeper effect itself.

### Key takeaway

Delayed persuasion after source-based discounting is more specific than repetition or any change over time.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-30, approximately 00:15:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 17 - Resisting persuasion

### What the slide shows

The outline names forewarning and inoculation under preventing persuasion.

### Detailed notes

People can prepare for an influence attempt rather than encounter it unprepared. Forewarning alerts them that persuasion may occur. Inoculation goes further by presenting a mild challenge and creating an opportunity to defend the position. The next two slides distinguish these related strategies. Neither guarantees resistance, and they should not be treated as the same intervention.

### Key takeaway

Alerting someone to persuasion and rehearsing a response are related but distinguishable.

## Slide 18 - Forewarning

### What the slide shows

A definition describes advance notice of a persuasive message and an opportunity to prepare. A sports banner gives a playful visual example of a warning.

### Detailed notes

Forewarning makes a future influence attempt salient before the person encounters it. The instructor uses health-class scenarios and preparation for peer pressure: someone can anticipate a request, think about their own boundaries, and plan a response.

Advance notice can change how a later invitation is interpreted. Learning that a social invitation may contain a gambling-related persuasion attempt, for example, can make the listener evaluate it more cautiously. A warning by itself does not mean the listener has already practised resisting a weak argument. That additional element is central to the inoculation example on the next slide.

The point is preparation for an influence attempt, not simply any prediction that the future will be difficult.

### Key takeaway

Forewarning alerts the recipient to an upcoming attempt to influence them.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-30, approximately 00:30:00-00:45:00._

## Slide 19 - Inoculation

### What the slide shows

A definition describes building resistance by mildly attacking an attitude position. A vaccination photograph illustrates the inoculation metaphor.

### Detailed notes

Inoculation gives a person a manageable challenge to a position and an opportunity to formulate or practise a defence. In the lecture’s role-play examples, a person encounters a mild version of pressure to act against a chosen boundary and rehearses how to respond. The initial challenge is deliberately weaker than the later influence attempt.

A school anti-bullying role-play illustrates the difference from simple forewarning: “Someone may pressure you” provides notice; actually practising a response to a simulated challenge adds inoculation. The aim is to prepare an active response, not overwhelm the person with a powerful attack.

The vaccination image is a metaphor for preparing defences. This slide makes a psychological claim about persuasion, not a claim about the medical effects of vaccines.

### Key takeaway

A mild challenge plus an opportunity to defend a position can prepare resistance to later persuasion.

## Slide 20 - Psychological reactance

### What the slide shows

The definition describes a strong response when expected freedom of choice feels threatened. An image emphasizes autonomy.

### Detailed notes

A persuasion attempt can backfire when it feels like an attempt to remove choice. Reactance is motivated resistance to that threatened freedom. A person may reject the advocated position or become more attracted to the restricted alternative. Disagreement alone is insufficient: the threatened-choice element distinguishes reactance from a simple difference of opinion.

The lecture illustrates this with an increasingly controlling argument, overly restrictive rules, and a worker who quits after feeling unable to exercise any control. Political examples are presented as the instructor’s interpretations of social reactions, not evidence of every person’s motives.

In the parent–adolescent example, progressively tighter control may produce rebellion rather than compliance. This does not establish that all rebellion has one cause. It explains why stronger pressure need not produce more persuasion: beyond a point, it may change the listener’s goal to restoring autonomy.

### Key takeaway

Pressure can provoke opposition when the recipient experiences it as a threat to choice.

## Slide 22 - Mechanisms linking behaviour and attitude

### What the slide shows

An outline lists self-perception, insufficient justification, and overjustification.

### Detailed notes

This section asks what people conclude about why they acted. They can infer a preference from their own behaviour, overlook a situational influence, or overemphasize an external reason and discount their own interest. The instructor connects these processes to the broader wish for consistency. Keep the mechanisms distinct rather than treating each term as another name for dissonance.

### Key takeaway

## Slide 23 - Self-perception

### What the slide shows

The definition says people use behaviour to infer their thoughts and feelings. Overlapping circles labelled pattern, reality, and perception accompany a paraphrase: doing something may suggest liking it.

### Detailed notes

Self-perception means observing one’s own actions and using them as evidence about one’s attitudes. The lecture phrases the inference as: I chose to do this, so perhaps it matters to me or I wanted it. That inference can be mistaken if the situation supplied an important reason for the action.

An expensive concert ticket illustrates a possible retrospective interpretation: after committing money, the buyer may conclude that the performer must matter more than they had initially thought. The boot-camp discussion connects shared effort and discomfort to valuing a group and identifying with it. The instructor explains the latter partly through dissonance and effort justification.

Self-perception and dissonance are related in these examples but not identical. Inferring an attitude from behaviour does not by definition require an experienced conflict. Behaviour can also reflect constraint rather than preference, so “I did it” is not proof that “I freely wanted it.”

### Key takeaway

Behaviour provides evidence people may use to infer their own attitudes, but context affects the inference.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-30, approximately 00:45:00-01:00:00._

## Slide 24 - Insufficient justification and overjustification

### What the slide shows

Two definitions contrast overlooking the situation’s causal role with overattributing behaviour to the situation and discounting personal interest.

### Detailed notes

The course uses insufficient justification in two connected ways. In the general classroom discussion, it means overlooking how a social situation contributed to one’s behaviour. In the classic dissonance experiment on slide 28, a small external incentive supplies too little justification for an attitude-inconsistent action, increasing pressure to change the attitude. These uses should be connected explicitly rather than silently equated.

Overjustification in the general discussion means crediting the situation too much and discounting one’s own interest. For the same group outing, “This was entirely my character; the setting had no effect” overlooks social influence, whereas “I had no interest; the group completely made me do it” may overstate it. The behaviour alone does not identify which explanation the person is using.

The narrower intrinsic-motivation effect occurs when an expected external reward makes a previously enjoyed activity seem like something done for the reward. Slide 25 develops that case. Neither term excuses harmful behaviour or establishes that responsibility belongs entirely to a person or a group.

### Key takeaway

Distinguish the actual situational influence from the explanation given for the behaviour.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-30, approximately 00:45:00-01:13:06._

## Slide 25 - Expected rewards and intrinsic motivation

### What the slide shows

A bar graph plots the percentage of time playing with markers after expected, no, or unexpected reward conditions. The expected-reward group is lowest, at roughly 8%; the other two groups are around 17–18%.

### Detailed notes

The graph concerns later interest in an initially enjoyable activity. When a reward is expected for playing, people may interpret their participation as working for that reward. Once the incentive is absent, less spontaneous engagement can remain. No-reward and unexpected-reward conditions do not supply the same advance external explanation.

The lecture connects the pattern to monetizing a hobby or paying for an activity a person already likes. External compensation can change the meaning of the activity, substituting a reward-based reason for intrinsic interest. The important distinction is an expected, salient reward for an already interesting activity; the figure does not establish that every reward undermines every kind of motivation.

Read the outcome as later time spent playing, not as a direct measure of intelligence, learning, or reward size. The lecture discusses the pattern as overjustification and contrasts intrinsic with extrinsic motivation.

### Key takeaway

An expected reward can shift the perceived reason for acting and reduce later voluntary interest.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-30, approximately 01:00:00-01:13:06._

## Slide 26 - Cognitive dissonance

### What the slide shows

A definition describes discomfort when responses seem inconsistent. A balance-like diagram contrasts consistency with inconsistency and anxiety, and indicates routes to reducing dissonance.

### Detailed notes

Cognitive dissonance is discomfort associated with a perceived inconsistency among beliefs, attitudes, actions, or self-image. It creates motivation to restore consistency, but does not determine which component will change. A person may change behaviour, reinterpret it, change the attitude, or reduce the discrepancy’s importance.

The lecture repeatedly connects this to wanting actions to fit a valued self-image. The October 6 review describes relief from the uncomfortable state in terms of negative reinforcement: a response can be strengthened because it removes an unpleasant feeling. That does not mean every attitude change is caused by dissonance.

Distinguish discomfort from evidence that a belief is true. Rationalizing a choice can make a person feel better without making the choice better supported.

### Key takeaway

Dissonance motivates consistency, which may be restored through useful change or through rationalization.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-30, approximately 01:00:00-01:13:06; 2026-10-06, approximately 00:00:00-00:15:00._

## Slide 27 - Ways of reducing dissonance

### What the slide shows

A diagram begins with smoking while believing smoking can kill. Four branches show changing behaviour, explaining away the inconsistency, reducing its importance, and changing the attitude.

### Detailed notes

The diagram separates several responses to the same discrepancy. Changing behaviour means stopping the action that conflicts with the belief. Explaining away the inconsistency means constructing a reason why it supposedly does not apply: for example, calling oneself an occasional or social smoker. Reducing its importance means accepting a risk in general while telling oneself that other advantages or presumed personal protection make it less consequential. Changing the attitude means denying or minimizing the original belief that made the action inconsistent.

The lecture uses smoking and vaping to illustrate rationalization. Statements about good genes, exercise, or only occasional use are examples of how someone may reduce discomfort; they are not validated health protections. The course point is how a person explains away the inconsistency.

A question about these routes must provide enough information about the explanation used. Several rationalizations can overlap, so a bare statement that someone “feels better” does not identify a unique mechanism.

### Key takeaway

Different explanations can reduce the same uncomfortable discrepancy; identify what actually changed.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-30, approximately 01:00:00-01:13:06._

## Slide 28 - Festinger and Carlsmith: small and large payments

### What the slide shows

A bar graph compares reported enjoyment after $20 and $1 payments for saying a boring task was interesting. The $1 bar is higher; the $20 bar is near neutral or slightly negative.

### Detailed notes

Participants first performed a boring task, then were asked to tell another participant it was interesting. The contrast is the external justification for that counterattitudinal statement: a relatively large payment or a small payment. The later outcome is their own evaluation of the task.

A $20 payment supplied a ready explanation for the statement: it was made for the money. There was less need to privately re-evaluate the boring task. A $1 payment supplied less external justification. Interpreting the task as somewhat more enjoyable could help reduce the inconsistency between the statement and a self-image of being honest. Thus the smaller-payment condition produced the more positive later rating in this demonstration.

The result does not mean that participants enjoyed earning less money, that a smaller reward always improves performance, or that everyone necessarily changed equally. The study illustrates how an action and its justification can reshape the reported attitude toward a task.

### Key takeaway

Less external justification for an inconsistent action can produce more pressure for attitude change.

_Lecture source: 2026-09-30, approximately 01:00:00-01:13:06._

## Slide 29 - Postdecisional dissonance

### What the slide shows

A definition describes regret after an important decision. A check mark and a cross symbolize the chosen and rejected alternatives; a caption notes recommitting to the choice.

### Detailed notes

A consequential choice means gaining one option while giving up alternatives and resources. After choosing, a person can notice the forgone benefits and wonder whether another option was better. This creates postdecisional dissonance. It can be reduced by emphasizing the chosen option’s advantages and discounting rejected options.

The instructor uses a purchase, a college choice, and choosing one evening activity over another. The opportunity cost is the forgone alternative; that cost can contribute to dissonance but is not itself another name for cognitive dissonance.

Recommitting can make living with a choice easier. It can also bias later evaluation. Becoming more confident after choosing does not demonstrate that new objective evidence appeared or that the rejected options became worse.

### Key takeaway

After committing, people may re-evaluate alternatives to make their choice feel more defensible.

_Lecture source: 2026-10-06, approximately 00:00:00-00:15:00._

## Slide 30 - Spreading the alternatives after a choice

### What the slide shows

A line graph plots product ratings before and after choice. The chosen product rises from about 6.1 to 6.5; the rejected product falls from about 5.2 to 4.6.

### Detailed notes

The chosen option was already evaluated more positively before the decision. After the choice, the gap between the ratings becomes larger: the selected product is upgraded and the rejected product is downgraded. This is the pattern the lecture connects to reducing postdecisional dissonance.

Keep the comparison clear. The graph is about changed evaluations of products over time, not a measured change in the products’ physical quality. The person’s need to feel that the decision was sound can influence the later rating. The figure supports the illustrated pattern, not a claim that every purchaser or every decision will show the same numerical change.

### Key takeaway

A widening evaluation gap after commitment can reflect justification of the choice.

## Slide 31 - Foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face

### What the slide shows

Two definitions flank a figure at a doorway. Foot-in-the-door starts with a small accepted request; door-in-the-face starts with a large refused request before the smaller target request.

### Detailed notes

Foot-in-the-door first secures a small commitment, then asks for a larger related one. In the lecture’s coffee example, accepting a small helping role can support the self-perception “I am a helpful person,” making a later larger request feel more consistent. The petition example similarly connects a small public commitment to a later donation request. These are illustrations of a possible technique, not proof about every organization’s practices.

Door-in-the-face reverses the sequence: the persuader first asks for something large that is likely to be refused, then presents the smaller request actually desired. The second request can feel like a concession, a more reasonable contrast, or a way to relieve discomfort about refusing. The lecture contrasts a request for $1,000 followed by $100, and a highly demanding plan followed by a milder plan.

For a defensible distinction, track the sequence and whether the first request was accepted or rejected. Merely making two requests is insufficient. The instructor also mentions favours and hospitality alongside foot-in-the-door; reciprocity can contribute in those examples, so receiving a gift alone should not be treated as decisive evidence of an initial accepted request.

### Key takeaway

Foot-in-the-door builds from acceptance; door-in-the-face retreats after refusal.

## Slide 32 - Low-ball and bait-and-switch

### What the slide shows

Two bullets distinguish an initially appealing offer available only at a higher cost from an appealing offer that is unavailable and replaced by a worse alternative.

### Detailed notes

Low-ball persuades a person to commit to an attractive offer before revealing a higher cost or less favourable terms for that same offer. Bait-and-switch draws the person in with an attractive offer that is not available, then steers them toward a different, less attractive option. Both can exploit a person’s investment of time, effort, or commitment.

The lecture initially describes a sold-out advertised item while saying “lowball,” then explicitly clarifies that an unavailable item with an alternative is bait-and-switch; an available item that costs more is low-ball. Use that final distinction. A scenario about an advertisement saying “up to 70% off” is not automatically a clean low-ball case: the timing of commitment and the later terms matter.

The store-visit example connects these techniques to reluctance to leave empty-handed after travelling and browsing. That feeling may help explain continued engagement, but neither tactic guarantees a purchase.

### Key takeaway

For low-ball the same deal becomes costlier; for bait-and-switch the advertised deal is replaced.
