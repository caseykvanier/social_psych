## What this chapter is about

The chapter distinguishes intended harm from accidents, then considers what can influence aggression and what evidence would support an explanation.

## Slide 3 - Defining aggression

**What the slide shows:** The first topic is highlighted.

**Detailed notes:** The starting question is how to classify behaviour when the same physical event could have resulted from an accident, a rule-governed contest, or an attempt to harm.

**Key takeaway:** Ask what the person intended and whether the target wanted the harm.

## Slide 4 - What does aggression look like?

**What the slide shows:** An image shows two people sparring.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture used this ambiguous example to distinguish what looks aggressive from the course definition. Consenting participants may be practising or competing. The picture alone cannot establish intent and consent.

**Key takeaway:** Appearance alone is insufficient.

*Source: November 11, about 00:03–00:05.*

## Slide 5 - Helping and aggression

**What the slide shows:** Images of donation are contrasted with images of conflict and verbal harm.

**Detailed notes:** The contrast signals a move from other-oriented helping to intended harm. The form of the act matters less to the basic classification than what it is intended to accomplish.

**Key takeaway:** Aggression need not involve a physical injury.

*Source: November 11 opening; slide-specific boundary uncertain.*

## Slide 6 - Intent and an unwilling target

**What the slide shows:** The word intended is circled in the definition.

**Detailed notes:** Aggression is behaviour intended to harm another individual who does not wish to be harmed. Anger by itself is not behaviour, injury by itself does not imply intent, and a failed attempt can still be aggressive.

**Key takeaway:** Check the intention and target's willingness separately from the outcome.

*Source: November 11, about 00:05–00:06.*

## Slide 7 - The same outcome, different intent

**What the slide shows:** One collision icon represents intentional and accidental versions.

**Detailed notes:** An accident can cause severe harm without fitting the definition. The class considered how the same image could represent different circumstances.

**Key takeaway:** If the scenario gives only the outcome, intent is undetermined.

*Source: November 11, about 00:06–00:08.*

## Slide 8 - Why intent changes evaluation

**What the slide shows:** The image is repeated with a comparative judgment.

**Detailed notes:** People commonly evaluate deliberately causing harm more negatively than an otherwise similar accident. The contrast does not imply that negligent conduct is harmless or free from responsibility.

**Key takeaway:** A moral judgment and a classification of the behaviour are related but distinct.

*Source: November 11, about 00:07–00:08.*

## Slide 9 - Violence as a subset

**What the slide shows:** The slide narrows the category to extreme physical harm.

**Detailed notes:** Violence and aggression are not interchangeable here. Some aggression is nonphysical; serious accidental injury is not violence in the course's aggression classification merely because it is severe.

**Key takeaway:** Use the broader definition first, then ask if the narrower label applies.

*Source: November 11, about 00:07–00:08.*

## Slide 10 - Emotional and instrumental aggression

**What the slide shows:** A diagram branches into two motivational patterns.

**Detailed notes:** A distinction concerns whether the behaviour is driven mainly by an immediate emotional response or is used deliberately to achieve another goal. Motives can be mixed.

**Key takeaway:** Do not classify the motive solely from the visible form of harm.

*Source: November 11, about 00:08–00:12.*

## Slide 11 - Emotional or impulsive aggression

**What the slide shows:** Silhouettes arguing accompany low forethought and strong emotion.

**Detailed notes:** An impulsive response can still involve a momentary intent to harm. Lack of planning is not equivalent to an accident.

**Key takeaway:** The key contrast with instrumental aggression is dominant motive and forethought.

*Source: November 11, about 00:08–00:11.*

## Slide 12 - Instrumental or cognitive aggression

**What the slide shows:** A competition image accompanies the idea of planned action.

**Detailed notes:** Here, causing harm serves some other objective. Planning alone is not enough: the basic requirements of intended, unwanted harm still apply. Anger need not be present.

**Key takeaway:** Ask what the behaviour is a means to achieve.

*Source: November 11, about 00:08–00:12.*

## Slide 13 - Physical and nonphysical forms

**What the slide shows:** A second branching diagram separates physical from nonphysical forms, with verbal and relational branches.

**Detailed notes:** This classification concerns where or how the harm is directed, not when it was planned. A case can occupy one category on each dimension.

**Key takeaway:** Identify motive and form separately.

*Source: November 11, about 00:11–00:14.*

## Slide 14 - Physical aggression

**What the slide shows:** Contact sport photos illustrate direct bodily action.

**Detailed notes:** Physical harm is the form at issue. Rule-governed, consensual contact is not automatically aggression; the circumstances still matter.

**Key takeaway:** The injury alone is not conclusive.

*Source: November 11, about 00:11–00:14.*

## Slide 15 - Verbal aggression

**What the slide shows:** An illustration accompanies the nonphysical branch.

**Detailed notes:** Words can be used to intentionally harm. Mere disagreement or criticism is not necessarily aggression, and verbal harm can overlap with relational harm.

**Key takeaway:** Specify the intended effect of the words.

*Source: November 11, about 00:12–00:14.*

## Slide 16 - Relational aggression

**What the slide shows:** A pair on a bench and a text box represent harm to social ties.

**Detailed notes:** Relational aggression aims to damage relationships, group membership, or social standing. Being left out is not enough to infer aggression unless the purpose was to cause harm. Discussion of forms and gender concerned averages and socialization, not rules about individuals.

**Key takeaway:** Look for an aim to damage ties or standing.

*Source: November 11, about 00:13–00:20.*

## Slide 17 - Cyberbullying

**What the slide shows:** Words displayed on electronic devices illustrate a digital medium.

**Detailed notes:** Distance and possible anonymity can reduce awareness of effects or accountability; neither is required for harm online. Digital communication is a medium, so the underlying behaviour can still be verbal, relational, or both.

**Key takeaway:** Distinguish the medium from the form and motive.

*Source: November 11, about 00:19–00:24.*

## Slide 18 - Biology and emotion

**What the slide shows:** The second topic is highlighted.

**Detailed notes:** The next explanations concern the interaction of threat, bodily state, self-regulation, and context. None is sufficient to classify a particular action or make it inevitable.

**Key takeaway:** Use biological information as part of an explanation, not a verdict.

*Source: November 11, about 00:23–00:25.*

## Slide 19 - Why and when do people aggress?

**What the slide shows:** Images of anger and threat surround two questions.

**Detailed notes:** The question shifts from classification to conditions. The fact that someone could respond aggressively does not indicate how likely that response is in a particular setting.

**Key takeaway:** Identify triggers, alternatives, and constraints rather than treating aggression as inevitable.

*Source: November 11, about 00:23–00:25.*

## Slide 20 - Threat as a possible trigger

**What the slide shows:** Two threat images illustrate threatening situations.

**Detailed notes:** Threat can produce defensive arousal and a range of responses. A perceived threat does not dictate what someone will do; appraisals and available alternatives matter.

**Key takeaway:** A trigger can increase the possibility of a response without being its sufficient cause.

*Source: November 11, about 00:24–00:28.*

## Slide 21 - Fight or flight

**What the slide shows:** Two images contrast confronting and withdrawing.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture considered why perceived safety, expected costs, and norms can favour different responses to a threat. A norm about looking strong is not the same as actual safety or a recommendation to confront.

**Key takeaway:** Multiple responses can follow the same perception of threat.

*Source: November 11, about 00:26–00:29.*

## Slide 22 - MAOA and childhood maltreatment

**What the slide shows:** The graph compares lower and higher monoamine oxidase A activity over levels of childhood maltreatment; the lower-activity series rises more sharply.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture treated this as a gene–environment interaction: the relationship between maltreatment and outcomes differed across activity groups. A graph of group averages is not an individual prediction, and the pattern is not a single-gene explanation.

**Key takeaway:** Compare the slopes across environmental conditions rather than just the endpoints.

*Source: November 11, about 00:29–00:34.*

## Slide 23 - Amygdala and threat processing

**What the slide shows:** A labelled head diagram lists emotional and threat-related processes.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture identified a location error in the diagram and moved to the next image. The amygdala participates in learning and responding to emotionally important stimuli. It is not simply an aggression switch.

**Key takeaway:** Use Slide 24 for the intended location; distinguish threat processing from behaviour.

*Source: November 11, about 00:34–00:38.*

## Slide 24 - Amygdala location

**What the slide shows:** A second visualization highlights paired structures inside the brain.

**Detailed notes:** The two highlighted areas help correct the misleading location on the previous slide. The image illustrates a location; it cannot show how someone behaved or establish a causal process by itself.

**Key takeaway:** Treat the figure as a spatial aid, not as evidence that aggression comes from one isolated area.

*Source: November 11, about 00:34–00:38.*

## Slide 25 - Prefrontal cortex and inhibition

**What the slide shows:** A head diagram locates the prefrontal cortex and lists executive functions.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture distinguished a strong immediate response from the ability to pause, consider consequences, and regulate expression. Developing self-regulation helps explain why an impulse does not have to become behaviour.

**Key takeaway:** An impulse is not the same thing as acting on it.

*Source: November 11, about 00:38–00:42.*

## Slide 26 - Testosterone and direction of influence

**What the slide shows:** Arrows go both from testosterone toward behaviour and back again.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture emphasized that social activity or outcomes can also affect hormone levels. An association can reflect multiple causal directions and does not show that an individual's behaviour is determined by a hormone.

**Key takeaway:** Avoid assuming that a correlation identifies a one-way effect.

*Source: November 11, about 00:42–00:50.*

## Slide 27 - Serotonin

**Detailed notes:** The deck presents serotonin as a potential correlate of lower aggression. The recording discusses neurotransmitters earlier, but a separate explanation of this slide's specific claim is not clearly established.

**Key takeaway:** Do not infer an individual's action from a neurotransmitter level or treat this slide as clinical advice.

*Source: Slide-specific coverage uncertain.*

## Slide 28 - Alcohol and aggression

**What the slide shows:** The slide pairs increases in alcohol and aggression.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture used alcohol to discuss why the same person might respond differently to the same situation. Alcohol is not an automatic explanation for every act; the proposed mechanisms are on the next slide.

**Key takeaway:** Explain a mechanism rather than merely naming a substance.

*Source: November 11, about 00:50–00:52.*

## Slide 29 - Disinhibition and alcohol myopia

**What the slide shows:** Arrows lead from alcohol through weaker inhibition and narrowed attention.

**Detailed notes:** Weaker inhibition makes it harder to stop an immediate response. Myopia means attention is concentrated on prominent immediate cues, such as a provocation, at the expense of less salient cues, such as consequences or alternatives.

**Key takeaway:** The two pathways concern stopping a response versus what information receives attention.

*Source: November 11, about 00:51–00:53.*

## Slide 30 - Frustration

**What the slide shows:** A person facing a difficulty represents a blocked goal.

**Detailed notes:** Frustration is the negative experience of being impeded in pursuing something important. It can increase irritability but does not guarantee aggression.

**Key takeaway:** A blocked goal is a possible antecedent, not evidence of an intention to harm.

*Source: November 11, about 00:53–00:54.*

## Slide 31 - Discomfort, pain, and heat

**What the slide shows:** Three illustrations show sources of discomfort.

**Detailed notes:** Aversive conditions can raise irritability even without a deliberate provocation. A person who is uncomfortable may respond differently to an ordinary encounter, but context and regulation still matter.

**Key takeaway:** Distinguish background discomfort from another person's intention.

*Source: November 11, about 00:54–00:56.*

## Slide 32 - Reducing aggression

**What the slide shows:** Balanced stones introduce a question about regulation.

**Detailed notes:** The issue is which responses actually reduce subsequent aggression, not just which feel relieving in the moment.

**Key takeaway:** Ask what outcome was measured and when.

*Source: November 11, about 00:56–00:58.*

## Slide 33 - The catharsis prediction

**What the slide shows:** Steam pressure illustrates the idea of releasing aggression.

**Detailed notes:** Catharsis predicts that a less harmful aggressive outlet reduces later harmful behaviour. The analogy is a hypothesis; a feeling of release is not proof of the predicted behavioural effect.

**Key takeaway:** Separate immediate self-reported relief from later action.

*Source: November 11, about 00:57–00:58.*

## Slide 34 - Why venting is not the same as reducing aggression

**What the slide shows:** The slide rejects the catharsis prediction and lists arousal and reward.

**Detailed notes:** An aggressive outlet can maintain arousal or feel rewarding and thereby reinforce the response. A nonaggressive way to calm down is conceptually different from rehearsing aggression to get relief.

**Key takeaway:** Check the later behaviour, not only whether the person feels better immediately.

*Source: November 11, about 00:58–00:59.*

## Slide 35 - Social and situational influences

**What the slide shows:** The next topic is highlighted.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture shifts to how people learn what is acceptable and effective in a social setting.

**Key takeaway:** Distinguish learning a response from being biologically compelled to use it.

*Source: November 11, about 00:59–01:00.*

## Slide 36 - Where do response patterns come from?

**What the slide shows:** A family image introduces learned behaviour.

**Detailed notes:** People observe how problems are handled in their environment and notice what follows those actions. A model provides an example; consequences can make the response more or less likely to recur.

**Key takeaway:** Separate learning by observing from learning through personal consequences.

*Source: November 11, about 00:59–01:01.*

## Slide 37 - Observational and reinforcement learning

**What the slide shows:** A child appears to notice another individual's behaviour and its outcome.

**Detailed notes:** Observational learning can happen before the observer has acted. Direct reinforcement occurs when a consequence makes a person's own behaviour more likely. Seeing a model get rewarded also conveys information about likely results.

**Key takeaway:** Ask who acted, who observed, and who experienced the outcome.

*Source: November 11, about 00:59–01:02.*

## Slide 38 - Learning across childhood and adulthood

**What the slide shows:** Two panels contrast childhood and adult settings.

**Detailed notes:** Expectations about aggressive behaviour can carry forward when early patterns are rewarded, but new settings can change both consequences and available alternatives. An early pattern does not establish an inevitable outcome.

**Key takeaway:** Learning can persist or change with contingencies and opportunities.

*Source: November 11, about 01:01–01:04.*

## Slide 39 - Consequences and alternatives

**What the slide shows:** A list emphasizes promptness, consistency, certainty, justification, and an alternative behaviour.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture distinguished suppressing a behaviour from teaching what to do instead. If a response disappears only when an authority is present or if a useful alternative is missing, the underlying problem may remain. The slide is not a recommendation to harm or intimidate.

**Key takeaway:** A consequence alone does not teach an alternative response.

*Source: November 11, about 01:04–01:06.*

## Slide 40 - Media exposure and aggression

**What the slide shows:** Screens and arrows present a relation between exposure and aggression.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture distinguished changes in thoughts or very short-term responses from real-world violence. A diagram pairing two increases does not identify a causal effect, and exposure is not a uniform experience across people or media.

**Key takeaway:** Ask which outcome was actually measured before interpreting an association.

*Source: November 11, about 01:06–01:08.*

## Slide 41 - Accessibility of aggressive ideas

**What the slide shows:** A thought bubble illustrates an aggressive response coming to mind easily.

**Detailed notes:** Accessibility is about how readily an idea is retrieved. Frequently represented responses can become easier to think of without being acted on. Separate this from learning a specific action by copying a model.

**Key takeaway:** Being able to think of a response is not choosing it.

*Source: November 11, about 01:06–01:09.*

## Slide 42 - Stimuli and responses

**What the slide shows:** Two upward arrows juxtapose violent cues and responses without showing data.

**Detailed notes:** The lecture cautioned against reading mental availability as an inevitable behavioural effect. Compare what happens when a cue makes thoughts accessible with what happens when people must make an actual choice.

**Key takeaway:** An arrow is a proposed relationship, not a measure of effect size or causality.

*Source: November 11, about 01:06–01:10; overlapping discussion.*

## Slide 43 - Modelling

**What the slide shows:** An image and arrow indicate imitation; the object shown is illustrative.

**Detailed notes:** A representation can supply a behavioural script or portray a behaviour as effective. The process concerns what is learned from the example, not a claim that viewers copy everything they see.

**Key takeaway:** Distinguish imitating an observed action from an idea merely coming to mind.

*Source: November 11, about 01:08–01:11.*

## Slide 44 - Desensitization

**What the slide shows:** An unmoved observer illustrates a diminished response to repeated stimuli.

**Detailed notes:** Desensitization concerns reacting less strongly after repeated exposure. That is not the same as having a thought more readily available or imitating a model. A smaller reaction also does not prove a person will aggress.

**Key takeaway:** Identify the changing response: emotional sensitivity, ease of recall, and imitation are different outcomes.

*Source: November 11, about 01:10–01:12.*
