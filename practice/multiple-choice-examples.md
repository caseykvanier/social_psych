# Three worked examples for MC study agents

These examples are adapted from the September 18 agent-generated practice bank, not from real tests. Option order is varied and feedback is edited for clarity and accuracy. They are authorized public illustrations of the [agent guide](multiple-choice-agent-guide.md), not a comprehensive practice set or a description of the entire assessment. Their intended difficulty has not been calibrated using student performance.

Try each question before opening its explanation. Agents should learn the design principles, then create different source-grounded situations.

## MC-01 — Classifying an added unpleasant stimulus

**Intended level:** Application.

**Learning goal:** Apply a mechanism using the consequence and the later change in behaviour.

A museum adds an unpleasant alarm sound after visitors touch protected displays. Touching the displays becomes less common. Which classification best fits the alarm?

A. Negative punishment

B. Negative reinforcement

C. Positive punishment

D. Positive reinforcement

<details>
<summary>Answer, feedback, and source</summary>

**Correct answer: C.** Adding an unpleasant stimulus is positive punishment when it reduces the future probability of the behavior.

- **A:** Incorrect. The consequence adds an unpleasant stimulus rather than removing something desirable.
- **B:** Negative reinforcement removes something aversive and increases the target behaviour. Here a consequence is added and the behaviour decreases.
- **C:** Correct. An unpleasant stimulus was added, and touching the displays decreased.
- **D:** Reinforcement requires an increase in the target behaviour; here touching the displays decreases.

**Course evidence:** [Chapter 2, Slide 6 - Punishment](../chapters/02-social-learning-cognition/notes.md#slide-6---punishment). Check the chapter source manifest for coverage and draft review status.

</details>

### What an agent should learn from this item

All four alternatives are plausible learning mechanisms. The stem supplies both the added consequence and the later decrease in behaviour. The explanation separates addition/removal from increase/decrease rather than relying on how unpleasant the consequence sounds.

**Generated-practice provenance:** `sp-a26-ch02-pool25-concept-04-0010` (2026-09-18 revision; adapted for this public example).

## MC-02 — Limits of a negative-reinforcement inference

**Intended level:** Application with an evidence-inference demand.

**Learning goal:** Identify the additional observation needed to support a mechanism.

A student reports that studying sometimes reduces guilt about avoiding work. Which additional observation would be needed to support the claim that negative reinforcement is increasing the student’s studying?

A. The student believes academic success is personally meaningful but still avoids studying.

B. The student studies more often after experiencing relief from the guilt.

C. The student describes studying as morally responsible and guilt as morally bad.

D. The student feels guilty before studying but studies with the same frequency as before.

<details>
<summary>Answer, feedback, and source</summary>

**Correct answer: B.** Negative reinforcement is an increase in behaviour because something aversive is removed. Relief from guilt alone is insufficient without evidence that studying becomes more frequent.

- **A:** This shows a possible inconsistency, but not that studying increases because guilt is removed.
- **B:** Correct. Negative reinforcement requires both removal of an aversive feeling and an increase in the behaviour.
- **C:** Moral labels do not establish negative reinforcement; the key evidence is increased behaviour after removal of an aversive feeling.
- **D:** An unchanged frequency of studying does not show reinforcement, even if guilt is present or relief occurs.

**Course evidence:** [Chapter 3, Slide 11 - Self-discrepancy and cognitive dissonance](../chapters/03-the-self/notes.md#slide-11---self-discrepancy-and-cognitive-dissonance). Check the chapter source manifest for coverage and draft review status.

</details>

### What an agent should learn from this item

The learner must identify missing evidence rather than simply label a description. Reduced guilt alone does not establish an increase in studying. This adds a reasoning step without requiring an untaught concept or a longer story.

**Generated-practice provenance:** `sp-a26-ch03-pool25-concept-09-0010` (2026-09-18 revision; adapted for this public example).

## MC-03 — Testing two unsupported inferences

**Intended level:** Stretch.

**Learning goal:** Select separate evidence for two different inferences.

A workshop facilitator is certain that participants learned the material and predicts that most participants found the activities useful because the facilitator enjoyed leading them. Which evidence would most directly evaluate both inferences?

A. The facilitator’s confidence and a detailed description of the activities

B. The number of activities and the facilitator’s estimate of their difficulty

C. The facilitator’s memory of one enthusiastic participant’s comments

D. A learning measure and anonymous participant ratings of the activities

<details>
<summary>Answer, feedback, and source</summary>

**Correct answer: D.** The facilitator is making two different inferences: confidence about participants’ learning and an assumption of shared approval based on personal enjoyment. Separate outcome and participant evidence are needed to evaluate them.

- **A:** Incorrect. These provide more information about the facilitator’s perspective, not direct evidence of learning or participants’ views.
- **B:** Incorrect. Activity count and estimated difficulty do not directly measure learning or participants’ evaluations.
- **C:** Incorrect. One memorable comment cannot establish overall learning or majority approval.
- **D:** Correct. A learning measure addresses whether participants learned, while participant ratings address whether they found the activities useful.

**Course evidence:** [Chapter 2, Slide 12 - Common cognitive shortcuts and biases](../chapters/02-social-learning-cognition/notes.md#slide-12---common-cognitive-shortcuts-and-biases). Check the chapter source manifest for coverage and draft review status.

</details>

### What an agent should learn from this item

The learner must separate a claim about learning from a claim about participants’ views, then choose evidence for each. Confidence, a memorable reaction, and activity descriptions are tempting substitutes for direct measurement, but do not answer both questions.
